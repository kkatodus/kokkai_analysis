POLIS: composing personas in weight space

How do you simulate a politician who has barely said anything on the record? This is a guided tour of the actual code behind the POLIS paper — every concept paired with the lines that implement it, and every number taken from the project's own data files. If you have taken one machine-learning course and know roughly what a neural network and a language model are, §1 will get you the rest of the way.

How to read this §1–§14 build the method up from scratch; every unfamiliar term gets defined where it first appears, and there is a glossary at the end of §1. §15–§21 are the results, and they read like a plot: the method beats every weight-space baseline on 33 politicians, then ties a much simpler baseline nobody had run, then loses outright to the simplest method in the study — and then a one-hour control shows that all three of those results were measuring a choice made months earlier and never varied, the objective the anchor adapters were trained with. Fixing it reverses the two defeats. What survives is two findings that came from controls rather than comparisons: roughly three quarters of what any persona adapter delivers is not about the person at all, and whether the remaining quarter carries ideology is decided by that training objective. §22 steps out of the walkthrough voice entirely: an editorial assessment of what is fragile in this research and how to harden it. If you only read five sections, read §1, §15, §18, §19 and §22.

Written against the paper as submitted (main.tex, 25 pages) and the project's decision log as of 2026-08-06. Every result number here is read from artifacts/paper_numbers.json, the same file the paper's tables and figures are generated from.

1 · Primer: five ideas you need first

Everything in this paper follows from five ideas. None of them require more than first-year linear algebra. If they are already familiar, skip to §2.

① A model is a big pile of numbers

Qwen2.5-7B — the model used here — is about 7.6 billion numbers, organised into matrices. When you feed it text, it multiplies that text (as vectors) through those matrices and produces a probability for every possible next word. The numbers are called weights, and they are the entire model. Change them and you change what it says.

② Fine-tuning means nudging those numbers

To make a model imitate a specific person, you show it that person's text and adjust the weights so their sentences become more likely. The model does not gain a "Kishida module" — every relevant weight simply shifts a little. Write the finished weights as Wafter = Wbefore + ΔW. That ΔW ("delta W", the change) is the entire product of fine-tuning, and it is a matrix of the same shape as the original.

③ ΔW is a vector, so you can do arithmetic on it

This is the leap. If you flatten a ΔW into one long list of numbers, it is a vector — a point, or a direction, in a space with hundreds of millions of dimensions. And vectors can be added, scaled, and averaged.

So 0.5 × ΔWKishida + 0.5 × ΔWShiokawa is a perfectly well-defined thing to compute. Whether it means "halfway between those two politicians" is an empirical question — and it is the question the whole paper is testing. This idea is usually called a task vector.

④ "Weight space" is where those vectors live

Picture the base model sitting at the origin. Each fine-tuned politician is a point some distance away, in their own direction. Blend those directions and you land somewhere in between — and the set of all reachable blends is the span of your anchors.

Schematic — the real space has ~822 million dimensions, and no honest 2-D picture of it exists. What is real is the consequence: a target inside the shaded region can be reached by blending; one outside it cannot. §3 measures how often that actually happens.

⑤ Log-likelihood: scoring a guess

Finally we need a way to say whether one persona is better than another. A language model assigns a probability to every next word, so given a sentence a real politician actually said, you can ask: what probability did this model assign to those exact words?

Probabilities of long sentences are astronomically small, so we take logarithms and add them instead of multiplying — and then flip the sign so that smaller is better. That gives negative log-likelihood (NLL), measured in nats per word.

Low NLL = the model found the real sentence unsurprising = it is a good imitation. This is the score POLIS optimises, and it appears in every results table.

The whole paper in one sentence Fine-tune four data-rich politicians, treat their ΔW as vectors, and for a politician with almost no data, search for the blend of those four vectors that makes their handful of real sentences least surprising.

2 · The problem: you cannot fine-tune on data you don't have

"Silicon sampling" is the idea of using an LLM to stand in for a human respondent — ask the model to role-play a person and record its answers as if they were survey data. The failure mode is immediate and well-documented: ask a model to be a politician and it produces a caricature assembled from media coverage, not that person's actual positions.

Fine-tuning on the person's own words fixes this — if the words exist. In the Japanese Diet, a handful of party leaders have tens of thousands of transcribed utterances, while most backbenchers have almost none. That long tail is exactly who a representative simulation needs, and exactly where per-person fine-tuning is impossible.

The reframe: locate, don't train

POLIS treats ideology as a continuous space and the weight-changes from fine-tuning as composable vectors in that space. Train adapters only on the few data-rich politicians ("anchors"). For a sparse target, don't train anything — find the blend of anchors that best explains the handful of sentences the target did say. The target's data is used to locate a point, not to fit a model. With 4 anchors and 3 depth groups that's 12 numbers to determine, which is a very different statistical problem from fine-tuning billions of parameters on 30 sentences.

3 · Choosing the anchors — and the limit nobody can optimise away

Before any training happens, someone has to decide which four politicians become the anchors. This choice is more consequential than it looks: a blend of anchors can only ever express positions that lie between them. Pick four politicians who all agree with each other and there is nothing to interpolate.

The selection is automated. Every politician gets a speech count from their archive, and an ideological position from the project's existing scaling data — a file with 55 separate policy "axes" (immigration, defence, nuclear power, …), each placing politicians on a 1-D line.

4 · Building the data: session-grounded preference pairs

Before any modelling, we need training examples that capture how a specific politician actually talks. The trick here is session grounding: rather than isolated quotes, each example reconstructs the real conversational context from the Diet transcript.

For every real utterance by an anchor, one training instance is emitted:

The immediately preceding speech is always kept intact even when truncating, because it is usually the question being answered — drop it and the reply becomes incoherent.

The implementation uses Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite instead — cheaper, no GPU — and the source file says so in its own docstring rather than quietly diverging: "the DPO signal becomes 'prefer the real utterance over a capable general LLM's role-play' rather than 'over the policy's own.' Context is capped at ~1200 characters as a proxy for the trainer's 1024-token limit (Japanese runs roughly one token per character). In practice the resulting prompts are ~1,336 tokens at the median — a number that comes back to haunt the compute budget later.

5 · The two anchor objectives

Read this section knowing where it ends up Each anchor was eventually trained twice — once with DPO, once with plain supervised fine-tuning (SFT) — on the same politician, the same 1,500 pairs and the same adapter shape, differing in the loss function alone. That was not the plan. DPO was chosen early, fixed, and never varied, and for months every downstream comparison silently conditioned on it. It turned out to be the single most consequential choice in the project: §19 shows the two arms landing on opposite sides of a permutation test, and two of the results in §16 and §17 reverse when it changes. So read the DPO derivation below for what it is, and hold on to one property of it — the gradient vanishes once the preference margin is satisfied — because that is the mechanism the whole reversal turns on.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is how each anchor adapter was trained first. The setup: you have pairs of responses where one is better than the other, and you want the model to prefer the better one. The classic recipe for this is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), which does it in two stages — first train a separate "reward model" to score responses, then use reinforcement learning to push the language model toward high-scoring output. DPO's contribution is a piece of algebra showing you can skip the middle step entirely: the two stages collapse into one loss function you can minimise with ordinary gradient descent.

L = − log σ( β · [ log πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] )

Reading it piece by piece:

  • πθ is the model being trained; πref is a frozen copy of where it started.
  • yw ("win") is the real utterance; yl ("lose") is the caricature.
  • Each ratio asks: has this response become more likely than it was before training? Dividing by the reference is what keeps the model from wandering off — it can only be rewarded for moving relatively.
  • The difference of the two ratios is a score: high when the model has raised the real utterance and lowered the caricature. σ is the sigmoid, the S-shaped function that squashes any number into the range (0, 1) — so the score becomes a probability, and taking −log of it makes it a loss that is small when the probability is near 1.
  • β ("beta") is a dial: small β lets the model drift far from where it started, large β keeps it close. Set to 0.1 here.

Two diagnostics get reported during training, and both appear in the project logs: rewards/accuracies (how often the real utterance scores above the caricature — reached 1.0) and rewards/margins (by how much — reached 7–9). The four 7B anchors all trained cleanly to these values.

A batch size of one is not a typo. DPO holds two models in memory (the policy and its frozen reference) and processes two completions per example, so the memory cost per step is roughly four times ordinary fine-tuning. Accumulating gradients over 8 steps recovers an effective batch of 8 without ever holding 8 examples at once. This is the standard toolkit for training a 7B model on hardware that has no business training one.

The other arm: plain supervised fine-tuning

SFT is the ordinary thing, and its loss fits on one line: maximise the log-probability of what the politician actually said, given the context. No reference model, no pairs, no rejected side — the same 1,500 instances with the third column thrown away.

LSFT = − log πθ(yw | x)

Put the two side by side and one difference matters more than it looks. SFT's gradient points at the politician's own distribution and keeps pointing there for the whole run: the only way to reduce the loss is to make their words more likely. DPO's gradient points at a margin — once the real utterance is comfortably more likely than the caricature, σ saturates, the gradient vanishes, and training stops. Any direction in weight space that beats the caricature satisfies it, and most of those directions carry no information about the politician at all.

That is an argument, not a measurement. §19 is the measurement, and it comes out where the argument says it should.

Careful — a distinction that runs through everything after §15 High DPO margins mean the adapter reliably separates real from fake. That is not the same as having learned ideology. At 0.5B the adapters largely captured register — the cadence and vocabulary of Diet speech, independent of what is being argued — which is why one anchor kept winning as a generic "fluent-parliamentarian" backbone regardless of the target's party. Learning how a politician talks and learning what a politician believes are separable, and §18 ends up putting a number on the split: about three quarters register, one quarter identity, with §19 showing that the quarter exists only under one of the two objectives above.

6 · LoRA and the idea of a "task vector"

Fine-tuning every weight of a 7B model per politician is unaffordable. LoRA freezes the base model and learns a low-rank correction instead. For a weight matrix W ∈ ℝout×in, it learns two thin matrices A ∈ ℝr×in and B ∈ ℝout×r with r = 16, and the effective weight becomes W + ΔW where:

ΔW = (α / r) · B · A

The trick is one of shape. Multiplying an out×r matrix by an r×in matrix gives an out×in result — the same shape as W — but you only ever stored the two thin factors. Rank r is the width of the bottleneck: everything the adapter learned has to squeeze through 16 dimensions. α/r is a fixed scaling constant from the config (α=32, r=16, so the scale is 2).

At the shipped r=16 that is 10.1 million stored numbers standing in for 822 million of full-rank change — an 81× compression. On disk adapter_model.safetensors is 20 MB, versus ~15 GB for a full copy of the model; the 522 MB you actually have to ship to a GPU box is four of those plus their training checkpoints and tokenizer files. Note also that k_proj and v_proj are 512×3584 rather than 3584×3584: Qwen2.5 uses grouped-query attention, a memory-saving design where several attention heads share one set of keys and values, so those two projections are already narrow — which is why the 112 modules only come to 822M rather than the 1.4B you would get from four square projections per layer.

Here is the conceptual leap the whole paper rests on. Once training is done, ΔW is just a matrix — a direction in weight space that means "be more like Kishida." It can be added, scaled, and combined like any vector. This is the task vector view from §1③, and it is what makes "average two personas" a meaningful operation rather than a category error.

Note what the merge code does first, before anything else: it multiplies the factors back out. LoRA's compression is a storage convenience; the arithmetic happens in the full-size space.

Does the "space" actually have room?

A blend of anchors can only express positions the anchors span. If all four ΔW pointed the same way, there would be nothing to interpolate — one shared direction with slightly different magnitudes. So the first thing the project checked was the pairwise cosine similarity between anchor task vectors (1.0 = identical direction, 0 = orthogonal).

Measured at 7B across 822,083,584 adapted parameters per anchor. The 0.9 threshold is the script's own kill switch — anchor_delta_cosine.py:96–106 prints [WARN] near-collinear anchor pairs and tells you to revisit the design. Everything here is far below it, so the anchors genuinely span a space.

The interesting part Look at the structure, not just the magnitudes. Every left-party pair (≈0.088) is more similar to each other than any of them is to the LDP anchor (≈0.064). At 0.5B these numbers were uniform regardless of party — norms also roughly double with scale (0.76–0.82 at 0.5B, 1.25–1.33 at 7B). Ideology is showing up as geometry; note that this is 3 pairs against 3 pairs, so it is suggestive, not a significance test.

Hold on to it through §18 and §19. For a long stretch of this project it looked like the strongest pro-ideology evidence anywhere in it and like the one finding that did not survive contact with the downstream measurements: the anchor directions clustered by party, and nothing built out of them behaved like it knew anyone's party. §19 reconciles the two. The behavioural signal was there all along and was being destroyed by the anchors' training objective — and the cosine structure above was measured on the arm where it survives.

7 · DARE: drop most of the vector, keep the meaning

When you add several task vectors together they interfere — unrelated coordinates pile up into noise that degrades the model. DARE (Drop And REscale) is a surprisingly blunt fix: randomly delete ~90% of each task vector's entries, then multiply the survivors by 1/density to compensate.

keep with probability d · then multiply by 1/d

Why this doesn't destroy the adapter: the rescaling preserves the expected value of every entry (drop 90%, multiply the rest by 10, and the average is unchanged). Fine-tuning deltas turn out to be highly redundant, so a random 10% carries nearly the same information — while the collisions between different anchors become far rarer, since two sparse vectors rarely have non-zeros in the same place. 

mask = (torch.rand(delta.shape, generator=gen) < self.density)
delta = torch.where(mask, delta / self.density, torch.zeros_like(delta))

Why generator=gen is load-bearing The mask is drawn from a seeded generator fixed once at construction. If the mask were redrawn on each merge, the same coefficients would give a different result every time — the optimiser in §11 would be chasing a moving target it cannot model. Freezing the randomness makes the objective a deterministic function of the coefficients. This is a general principle worth absorbing: stochastic components must be frozen before you optimise through them.

8 · TIES: settle the disagreements by vote

DARE reduces collisions but doesn't resolve them. When two anchors do both want to change the same weight in opposite directions, naive averaging cancels them into mush — a weight that means something to both anchors ends up meaning nothing.

TIES resolves this per-coordinate with an election. For each individual weight: sum the (already coefficient-weighted) contributions to decide a winning sign, discard every anchor that disagrees with that sign, and average only the survivors. A coordinate ends up decisively positive or decisively negative — never averaged into zero. 

def _ties(deltas):
    """TIES disjoint merge over already-weighted deltas [n_anchors, out, in]."""
    elected = torch.sign(deltas.sum(dim=0))                  # winning sign per weight
    agree   = (torch.sign(deltas) == elected) & (deltas != 0)  # who voted with it
    kept    = torch.where(agree, deltas, torch.zeros_like(deltas))
    count   = agree.sum(dim=0).clamp(min=1)
    return kept.sum(dim=0) / count                        # mean of the winners only

Note clamp(min=1) — it guards the divide-by-zero when a coordinate is zero in every anchor (very common after a 90% drop). And the average is over agreeing anchors only, so a coordinate touched by one anchor keeps that anchor's full magnitude rather than being diluted by three zeros.

9 · Layer groups: the knob that makes this a search problem

So far a merge needs one weight per anchor. POLIS's contribution is that one number per anchor is not enough. Transformer layers specialise: earlier layers handle more lexical and syntactic structure, later layers more abstract and task-specific content. A persona is not uniformly distributed across depth — so the amount of each anchor you want to inject should vary by depth.

Layers are partitioned into G contiguous depth groups, and the coefficient becomes a matrix C ∈ ℝK×G — one value per (anchor, group) pair. With 4 anchors and 3 groups that is 12 free parameters, the entire "model" being fit to the sparse target. 

def _group_of(self, mod_path):
    layer = int(m.group(1)) if m else 0
    # contiguous depth partition: floor(layer / ceil(n_layers / n_groups))
    per = -(-self._n_layers // self.n_groups)  # ceil div
    return min(layer // per, self.n_groups - 1)

Why custom code was needed The standard library (PEFT) offers add_weighted_adapter(combination_type="dare_ties"), but it only supports one global weight per adapter. The per-depth coefficient matrix is precisely what it cannot express, so the merge is reimplemented as direct tensor arithmetic. That is the reason merge_layer_group.py exists at all.

10 · The objective: how well does this persona predict real sentences?

To search over C we need a number that says how good a merge is. POLIS uses negative log-likelihood of the target's real utterances, given their real session context. In plain terms: show the merged model the room, and ask how surprised it is by what this person actually said. Lower is better. 

def mean_nll(self, instances):
    for prompt, cont in instances:
        ids_p = self.tok(prompt, ...).input_ids       # session context
        ids_c = self.tok(cont, ...).input_ids         # what they really said
        ids = torch.cat([ids_p, ids_c], dim=1)
        logits = self.model(ids).logits[:, :-1]
        logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)
        tok_logp = logp.gather(-1, ids[:, 1:].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)[0]
        n_ctx = ids_p.shape[1] - 1
        tot += -tok_logp[n_ctx:].mean().item()        # ← score ONLY the reply

Three details that are easy to skim past but matter:

  • logits[:, :-1] and ids[:, 1:] — the standard off-by-one of causal LMs. Position t's logits predict token t+1, so predictions and targets must be shifted against each other.
  • gather picks out the log-probability the model assigned to the token that actually occurred, from a distribution over the whole vocabulary.
  • tok_logp[n_ctx:] — only the reply is scored. Including the context would swamp the signal with how predictable other people's speeches are, which has nothing to do with the target.

NLL is measured in nats per token. The gaps this paper reports are around 0.02 nats, which sounds like nothing, and in absolute terms it is. §13 and §15 explain why the consistency of that gap across folds carries the result rather than its size.

11 · Bayesian optimization: searching when every guess is expensive

Now the search. We need the C that minimises held-out NLL, and we face a specific situation: the space is only ~12-dimensional and continuous, but each evaluation is expensive — a full re-merge plus dozens of forward passes through a 7B model. We can afford tens of evaluations, not millions. No gradients are available (the merge involves sign elections and hard masking).

That combination — expensive, low-dimensional, gradient-free — is the textbook case for Bayesian optimization.

How it works

BO keeps a probabilistic model of the objective, called a surrogate — here a Gaussian process. A GP predicts, for every unevaluated point, both a mean guess and an uncertainty, and its uncertainty is exactly zero where you have already measured. Then an acquisition function chooses where to look next by balancing:

  • Exploitation — go where the surrogate predicts a good value.
  • Exploration — go where the surrogate is unsure, since something better might hide there.

Every piece is now on the table. This section follows a single BO trial through the actual call stack, so you can see how the sections connect. Step through it — each stage names the function that runs and what it hands to the next one.

Two things about this loop are worth naming explicitly, because they are what make the method affordable at all.

Nothing is trained, and nothing is reloaded. The model is loaded once. Every trial rewrites weights in place and rewrites them again next trial. Compare that to the obvious alternative — fine-tune the target 40 times with different settings — and the cost difference is the entire reason POLIS can run on 30 utterances.

The expensive part moved. At 0.5B the forward passes dominated. At 7B the re-merge does: writing ΔW for 4 anchors × 822M adapted parameters, every trial. That inversion is why the project's own runbook insists on a timed 5-trial calibration before committing to a full run — the old timings predict nothing.

13 · Nested cross-validation: not fooling yourself

Here is a subtle trap. If you tune 12 coefficients to minimise NLL on 30 sentences and then report that NLL, the number is meaningless — you optimised it directly. A held-out split fixes that, but with only 30 sentences the split is small and the estimate is noisy and luck-dependent.

Nested cross-validation is the clean answer. Split the budget into k folds. Hold one fold out, run the entire optimisation on the rest, then score on the untouched fold. Rotate through all folds and aggregate. Every fold's score comes from coefficients that never saw it.

The design detail that makes it fair Every baseline is rotated through identical folds — including best_single, which re-picks its winning anchor using only that fold's dev data. A baseline allowed to choose using the test fold would be getting information POLIS never had.

Paired vs unpaired — the statistic that decides the paper

Because every method is scored on the same five folds, the results are paired, and that changes which number you should look at. Here is the distinction, because it is the difference between "this works" and "this is noise" in §15.

Suppose method A scores [2.09, 2.24, 2.29] and method B scores [2.11, 2.25, 2.31]. Each list has a spread of about ±0.09 — far bigger than the ~0.02 gap between the two methods. Reported as "2.21 ± 0.09 vs 2.22 ± 0.09" the difference looks hopeless, buried in noise.

But look down the columns instead of across them. Fold 1: A wins by 0.02. Fold 2: A wins by 0.01. Fold 3: A wins by 0.02. The ±0.09 spread is not measurement error at all — it is some folds simply containing harder sentences than others, and both methods face exactly the same hard sentences. Subtract per fold and that shared difficulty cancels out, leaving [0.02, 0.01, 0.02]: a spread of ±0.005 around a mean of 0.017.

Rule of thumb When two methods are evaluated on the same data, always subtract first and average second. Averaging first throws away the pairing and inflates your apparent noise by whatever the variation between test items happens to be — which here is roughly tenfold. This mistake was actually made and caught mid-session on the real results; the unpaired reading said "inside noise", the paired reading said "5 of 5 folds".

14 · Scoring a survey — and a trap worth internalising

NLL is an internal proxy: it only says the model predicts the target's words well. The paper's external metric is different and deliberately untouchable — real politicians answered the UTAS academic survey, a set of policy statements each rated on a 5-point Likert scale from "agree" to "disagree", and those answers are never used in training or in the optimiser. Nothing in the pipeline can game them.

This section is how the survey is administered and scored. What it measured, and why that turned out to be the hardest question in the project, is §20.

The length bug

The natural implementation is to sum log P over the answer's tokens and softmax the sums. This is broken, and understanding why is worth the detour.

Every token's log-probability is negative. So a longer answer accumulates a more negative total simply for being longer. In this survey the options are 「そう思う」 (short) through 「どちらかといえばそう思う」 (much longer) — so a softmax over summed log-probs collapses onto the shortest option almost regardless of content. You measure string length, not belief.

The fix is to divide by token count, giving average per-token log-probability:

The expectation — Σ i·pi, each possible answer multiplied by its probability — is the payoff of scoring this way. It places the persona continuously on the 1–5 scale rather than forcing a discrete pick, so "leans agree" and "strongly agrees" are distinguishable. You then compare that number to the politician's real coded answer and average the gap across all 33 items, which gives mean absolute error (MAE) — literally "on average, how many scale points off were we". MAE of 0 is perfect; MAE of 1 means you are typically one Likert step out.

The second scorer, and why there had to be one

Length-normalising fixes the bias toward short options but introduces a subtler problem. After dividing by token count, all five options end up with per-token log-probabilities sitting in a narrow band — they are all fluent Japanese phrases, so none of them is very surprising to the model. Softmax over five near-identical numbers gives five near-equal probabilities, and the expectation Σ i·pi of a near-uniform distribution over 1–5 is… almost exactly 3. The metric collapses toward the midpoint whatever the model actually believes.

The alternative is numeric-label scoring: don't score the phrase 「そう思う」, score the single token "1", and let the system prompt carry the mapping. One token per option, so length normalisation is moot, and the five candidates are genuinely distinguishable to the model.

15 · Results: does it work?

Everything up to here was development work done at 0.5B — a model small enough to run on an 8 GB laptop GPU, which is the only reason the pipeline could be debugged at all. But a 0.5B model is barely coherent, and every negative result at that scale carries the same excuse: maybe the model is just too small. Settling the paper meant renting a 48 GB GPU and rerunning everything at 7B, 14× larger. That took five sessions over five days, and the ordering of configurations changed twice along the way.

The measurement is the nested cross-validation of §13: a real politician outside the anchor set, simulated by merging the four anchors, with only 30 of their utterances available and every score computed on a fold those 30 never tuned. How the 33 were chosen

Expanding cost no new training — a held-out target never gets an adapter, so adding one costs only its prompt/completion pairs and about ten minutes of GPU. Of the politicians with a complete survey vector and a speech archive, 550 were eligible and 517 had enough material for a 30/30 split. Thirty were sampled, five from each of six log-spaced bands of speech volume, plus the three originals.

The banding is the important design choice. A uniform random draw would have given one more average; sampling across the volume range gives a curve, so the method's advantage can be plotted against how prominent the target is rather than averaged over it. The 33 span 82 speeches (斎藤裕喜) to 9,139 (枝野幸男) across six parties: LDP 12, CDP/DPJ/DPP lineage 13, Ishin 3, JCP 3, Komeito 2.

That was too strong, and the counter-argument is simple: the optimiser tunes on 30 utterances no matter how many the target has, so the method is low-resource either way. What the original three really failed to test was not data quantity but kind of politician — nationally prominent members give prepared set-piece speeches, while backbenchers ask committee questions, and those are different prediction problems. Tuning does the work

The uniform all-ones merge is dramatically worse than not merging at all — 2.5558 against a base of 2.3613 on SFT anchors, and 2.7689 on DPO anchors. Untuned task vectors overshoot and corrupt the model. Every bit of the merge's advantage comes from choosing coefficients, which is the cleanest possible evidence that the optimisation stage is load-bearing rather than decorative. It is also why the coefficient bounds in §11 have to reach down to zero: the search's main job is turning things off.

And the amount it buys is strikingly stable. BO vs best-single is +0.0299 on the 33 SFT-anchor targets, +0.0286 on a 5-target SFT pilot, and +0.0265 on 33 DPO-anchor targets: three estimates of the search's contribution, agreeing at ~0.027 across anchor families that differ in everything else. Whatever the anchors carry, tuning how much of each to inject at each depth contributes the same amount.

Nested 5-fold CV on three targets: the tuned merge improved on best-single in 15/15 folds (+0.072 to +0.092 nats) and on base in 14/15, against a uniform merge more than a full nat worse than base. Two things from that scale shaped the main study — the uniform-merge failure established that the contribution of tuning was a question to be tested rather than assumed, and best-single picking the same anchor in all 15 folds regardless of party was the register effect of §18, visible two months before anyone interpreted it as such.
Where this leaves the NLL story Against every weight-space alternative the method works, on 33 politicians, at main scale, on 30 sentences each, with no decay as the target gets more obscure. The next two sections are the two baselines that don't edit weights at all — and on the first anchors trained, they beat it.

16 · The baseline that nearly killed it — and what happened when they were stacked

Every baseline in §15's first three rows edits the weights. There is an obvious alternative that doesn't: just put the target's sentences in the prompt. Take the same 30 utterances the optimiser tunes on, paste them in front of the question as examples, and ask the base model to continue. No training, no adapters, no merging, no search. This is in-context learning (ICL), and it is the cheapest thing anyone would try before reading the paper.

The project's own falsification criterion This baseline was written into the original spec as one of seven required comparisons, with an unusually honest label attached: "the cheapest strong competitor; if it matches POLIS the method is unnecessary." The paper's central hypothesis claims the merge beats prompting. Four of the seven baselines had been implemented. This was not one of them, and it had never been run.

(It is stated in the paper as "specified in advance as a falsification criterion", not as pre-registered. Nothing was lodged in a public registry — it was written into the project's own spec before the run. "Pre-registered" is a term of art and claiming it would have been wrong.)

The result, first time

It was run with the n=33 batch, same folds, same protocol, on the anchors that existed at the time — the DPO ones. The merge beat it on 17 of 33 targets — mean −0.0343, median +0.0050, 80 of 165 folds. That is a coin flip, and by the criterion's own terms the method was unnecessary.

Same 33 targets, sorted, both anchor arms. The DPO arm (grey) is centred on nothing and enormous in both directions; the SFT arm (gold) is what the same comparison looks like once the anchors are fixed — 27 of 33 above zero, mean +0.0580, p = 3.2×10⁻⁴. The change is entirely in the anchors: identical folds, identical prefix, identical ICL scores.

Before believing a null, check the plumbing. The demonstration prefix is built from the dev half of each fold and the model is scored at restored base weights, so ICL never sees the test fold. It was not leakage. It was a real tie — against anchors that turned out to be carrying almost nothing about the politicians they were trained on.

Why the variance is so large — a mechanism, not a shrug

The interesting part is that the mean and the median disagree in sign. The merge won slightly more often than it lost, but when it lost it lost enormously: three targets account for most of the negative mean, each around −0.33 nats, an order of magnitude bigger than any of the merge's wins.

The explanation is measurable. For each target, compute how similar their own 30 utterances are to each other — call it self-similarity: 

corr(within-target utterance similarity, ICL gain over base) = +0.787
corr(within-target utterance similarity, BO vs ICL)          = -0.702

Those are strong correlations. When a politician says nearly the same thing every time, 24 examples in the prompt approach a lookup table — the held-out utterance is close to something already in the context window, and prompting wins by a landslide. The three big losses are exactly the repetitive speakers: 田野瀬 at 0.55 self-similarity and 手塚 at 0.40, against about 0.18 for a typical target.

It is tempting to stop there and call the tie an artefact of two weird politicians. It isn't. Drop the two most repetitive targets and the mean moves from −0.034 to −0.015 with the merge at 17 of 31 — still parity, just less dramatic parity. And 3022 津島 posts a large ICL win at perfectly ordinary self-similarity, so repetition is the main mechanism but not the only one.

The mechanism outlives the tie. On the final SFT anchors the merge wins overall, but the 120–250 band — where the boilerplate speakers live — is still the one band where prompting wins prefix-free (−0.0624), and 1543 手塚 alone is −0.1833. This is a property of the individual politician, not of their resource level.

What a tie licenses you to say — the interim position

Pause here, because for a day this was the result, and what you are allowed to write down at this moment is a genuinely delicate question. There is a real line between an honest framing and a rescue, and it is worth seeing where it falls before the next experiment moves it.

Fair to say

The two methods have very different costs. ICL pays a ~9,000-token prefix on every single inference, and needs the target's speeches available at serving time. The merge pays once and ships a 130 MB adapter. For deployment at scale that is a substantial difference, and it is a legitimate argument.

Not fair to say

That the fidelity claim survives. The hypothesis on record is that the merge beats prompting on how well it models the politician. It does not. A cost argument is a different claim, and swapping one for the other without saying so is how a null quietly becomes a positive result in a write-up.

Hold on to the right-hand card. Two experiments later the method is rescued twice over — it stacks with prompting, and once the anchors are fixed it beats prompting outright — and each time the temptation to reach for exactly that move comes straight back. The cost argument is still true, and it is still a different claim. It survives into the paper's discussion as a deployment point, which is where it belongs.

The experiment that decides it, and why it wasn't obvious

A tie between a weight-space method and a prompt-space method has two readings, and no comparison of the two alone can separate them:

  • They capture the same information by different means — in which case the merge is genuinely redundant.
  • They capture different information, and nobody ever tried stacking them — in which case combining should beat either.

So the deciding measurement is not the merge versus ICL at all. It is BO+ICL versus ICL: the merged weights with the demonstration prefix in front of them. If adding the merge on top of prompting improves things, the merge carries information the prefix does not, and the tie becomes a statement about prompting being a strong baseline rather than about the merge being pointless.

Note that this costs almost nothing to add: it is the same fitted merge scored with the prefix in front of each held-out prompt, reusing the merger state already applied. Two extra evaluation passes per fold and no extra optimisation trials. The reason it had not been done is not expense — it is that nobody had framed the two as things that could combine.

There is a control that has to run alongside it: best-single+ICL. Without it, a positive result would only show that some weight-space adaptation composes with prompting — not that the tuned merge specifically does. That distinction is the whole paper.

17 · The upset: plain fine-tuning wins — and then doesn't

Everything in this project rests on one founding assumption, stated back in §2: you cannot usefully fine-tune on 30 sentences, so you must locate the target in a space of pre-trained anchors instead. The original spec listed seven required baselines, and two of them test exactly that assumption — supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and DPO applied directly to the target's own 24 dev-fold utterances, no anchors, no merge. They were implemented. They had never been run on a GPU. They ran last, and for two days the founding assumption did not survive.

This section is the project's central wrong turn, told in the order it happened. If you only want the answer: the defeat was real, reproducible, 33/33 — and it was measuring the anchors' training objective rather than the merge. The measurement that showed this took one hour of GPU and is at the end of this section.

First: getting a fair number out of the competitor

SFT here means the ordinary thing: LoRA fine-tuning on the target's 24 utterances with a plain next-token loss — the method the paper exists to replace. Run with the script's default hyperparameters (learning rate 2e-4, 8 epochs), it scored 3.2745 against a base of 2.2684 — a full nat worse than not training at all, the signature of a 7B model memorising 24 examples.

The strawman trap That 3.27 could have gone straight into the paper as "SFT catastrophically overfits on sparse data, as expected" — confirming the founding assumption with a number produced by untuned defaults. A baseline run at bad hyperparameters is not a baseline; it is a strawman wearing one's clothes. The pilot grid on one target found a 50× spread in error across six hyperparameter cells. Your method gets its hyperparameters tuned; its competitor must get the same courtesy or the comparison is theatre.

The fair protocol landed on fixed hyperparameters (5e-5, 2 epochs), picked on one target and transferred unchanged to all 33 — no per-target selection, so no peeking asterisk. A check confirmed the fixed cell matches the per-fold-selected grid to the fourth decimal (2.1120 vs 2.1119), because that cell simply wins almost every fold.

18 · Most of persona adaptation is register, not identity

Every comparison so far is between methods: merge versus prompt versus fine-tune. This section asks a question that sits underneath all of them, and that no comparison between persona methods can answer. How much of what any of them delivers is specific to the person being simulated?

The control is embarrassingly simple, which is part of why it took months to run. Take one anchor adapter. Apply it alone, at coefficient 1.0. No merge, no tuning, no target data, not even the 30 utterances. Score it on all 33 targets. Then do that for all four anchors, so every target is scored against every anchor.

If adapters carry identity, the prediction is sharp: a target should be well served by an ideologically close anchor and badly served by a distant one, so the spread across the four anchors should be comparable to the gap they open against the base model.

Three checks that this is real

1 · It is not "the model already knows the famous one"

The obvious alternative explanation is that transfer works because the base model already knows these politicians from pretraining, so the adapters are nudging existing knowledge rather than teaching register. But 上田清司 (~4,000 speech records) transfers about as well as 岸田文雄 (~20,400, a sitting prime minister during the corpus period). A 5× difference in public profile makes essentially no difference to how well the adapter travels.

2 · A foreign anchor matches target-specific fine-tuning

From §17's probe: an untuned adapter trained on a different politician is level with LoRA fine-tuning on the target's own 24 utterances (3/5, +0.0047). At n=33 the same comparison is SFT vs best-single: +0.0003, 14/33, p = 0.49 — a clean null. Two sample sizes, same answer.

3 · It was visible at 0.5B before anyone interpreted it

In the development study, best-single selected the same anchor in all 15 folds regardless of the target's party. That was written down at the time as a curiosity. It was this effect, two months early and a 14× scale down.

Why this makes every margin in §15 interpretable

This is the honest answer to the obvious objection — why are all these effects around 0.03 nats?

Everything in §15 and §16 happens inside the small pale window. The tuned merge's +0.0299 over best-single is not competing against 0.2186 nats of persona fidelity; it is competing over the ~0.057 that anchor identity contributes at all.

Read against the right denominator, tuning the blend recovers roughly half of the available anchor-dependent signal — and it lands outside the anchors' own range, which is something a selection-only method cannot do by construction. The margins are small because the thing they are competing over is small, not because the method barely works.

The recommendation this generalises to Almost every persona-simulation paper benchmarks its method against other persona methods: prompting, fine-tuning, retrieval, a different merge. None of those comparisons can tell you whether any of the machinery is capturing the individual, because they all share the same register floor. A persona method should be measured against an untuned adapter trained on somebody else before it is measured against another persona method. If the foreign adapter matches it, most of what is being reported is genre.

The control costs one forward pass per target. It is the cheapest experiment in this entire project and it produced its largest number.

19 · The training objective decides whether identity survives

Three quarters of the effect is register. The remaining quarter — 0.0568 nats of spread across anchors — is either individual identity or it is noise, and §18 cannot tell which. Here is a test that can. If the anchor-dependent part is ideology, then the anchor a target prefers should track the target's politics.

Building a null that isn't a lie

The obvious null is 1/4 — four anchors, pick one at random. It is wrong twice over.

First, only 28 of the 33 targets are even eligible: the anchors cover LDP, JCP, SDP and the DPJ lineage, and the five Ishin and Komeito targets can never match by construction. No target is SDP, so 福島's family contributes no matchable target either.

Second — and this is the subtle one — a large party family holding a frequently-preferred anchor would match by construction. If 13 of the 28 targets are DPJ-lineage and the DPJ anchor happens to be a good all-round adapter, you get matches for free. Why the objective would do this

Go back to §5. SFT's gradient points at the politician's own distribution throughout training — the only way to lower the loss is to make their words more likely. DPO's objective is satisfied by separating the real utterance from the caricature, and its gradient vanishes once that margin holds. With a synthesised rejected side and limited data, the whole half-space of directions that beat the caricature is a solution, and most of that half-space carries no information about the politician.

Two independent observations agree with that reading. First, the appendix example makes the DPO negative concrete and shows it is not a strawman: the Gemini caricature of 塩川鉄也 on non-regular staff paid below minimum wage is fluent, on-topic, and argues the same side. What it lacks is procedure — it names no official, ties nothing to an ongoing policy movement, and ends in a generalisation where the real utterance ends in a specific institutional demand. Beating that does not require moving toward 塩川.

Second, the weight space says the same thing. The uniform merge — all four deltas added at coefficient 1.0 — scores 2.5558 on SFT anchors and 2.7689 on DPO anchors, against a base of 2.3613. DPO deltas interfere with each other far more destructively, which is what you would expect of task vectors pointing in directions unrelated to the politicians that produced them.

It is tempting to read that as early layers carrying lexical register and late layers carrying stance. Resist it — §15's granularity ablation shows that removing this structure entirely makes the merge better at equal search budget, so whatever the pattern is, it is not buying fidelity.

The methodological point, which is bigger than the result The anchors' objective was never a designed factor of this study. It became one because two sparse-target fine-tuning baselines were finally implemented and disagreed with each other — and that disagreement is the only reason the objective became visible as a confound at all.

Generalise it: where a pipeline contains a choice incidental to the hypothesis, a comparison that holds it fixed can invert when it is varied. And the baselines most likely to expose such a choice are precisely the ones that look least informative and get deferred. Run every baseline in the spec, early. The one you skip is the one that reinterprets everything else.

20 · The survey metric: an anatomy of a null, and its reframing

NLL is an internal measure — it says the merged model predicts the target's words well. The claim readers actually care about is bigger: does the simulated politician hold the right opinions? That is what the UTAS survey (§14) was for, and it is where the project spent most of its second day. The answer turned out to be no, and the way that was established is more instructive than the result.

Why a stupid baseline is not optional The midpoint dummy wins for a boring structural reason: real survey answers cluster near the middle, so "always say 3" is never far off. Any metric where a constant is competitive cannot distinguish a good model from a bad one, and you will not discover that by staring at your model's scores. Add the dumbest possible baseline before you interpret anything.

There is exactly one place where the models come out ahead, and it is the exception that proves the rule. 赤嶺's answers are bimodal — 14 of his 33 sit at one end of the scale and 8 at the other, with only 7 in the middle — so "always say 3" is an unusually bad guess for him. On the within-1 column (fraction of items landing within one scale point of the truth), every configuration beats the dummy, 0.455–0.667 against 0.333. The dummy is not strong; the models are just weaker than a lucky structural artefact almost everywhere else.

Step 2 · Separate "wrong persona" from "no persona"

Losing to a constant tells you the numbers are bad, but not why. There are two very different diagnoses, and they call for opposite responses:

Diagnosis A — wrong persona

The persona changes when you change who it is, but lands in the wrong place. The merge is doing something; it is doing it badly. Response: improve the merge.

Diagnosis B — no persona

The persona doesn't change at all when you change who it is. The survey answers are a property of the instrument, not of the politician. Response: stop reporting the metric — it cannot support a claim in either direction.

A one-target MAE cannot tell these apart. Distinguishing them needed a different experiment, and two facts made it cheap. First, the persona is conditioned on the politician's name alone — the system prompt says "you are Diet member X" and nothing else — so administering the survey needs no speech data, no adapter and no merge. Second, the survey wave contains 594 politicians with complete 33-item answer vectors, and only 3 had ever been used.

So: run the base 7B model as all 594 personas and administer the survey to each. Total cost, 10.7 minutes.

Step 3 · The population sweep

With 594 simulated answer vectors and 594 real ones, two questions become answerable.

Name-swap divergence. How much do the simulated answers move when the only thing you change is the name in the prompt — compared to how much the real politicians differ from each other? If simulations vary as much as real people do, the persona is carrying identity. If they barely vary, it isn't.

Take two politicians at random and average the gap between their answers, item by item, on the 1–5 scale. Real politicians differ by 1.13 scale points per item. Their simulations differ by 0.24 — a ratio of 0.209.

Not literally inert: of the 19,900 persona pairs, none produce identical answers. But changing the name moves the answers about a fifth as much as real politicians differ. Item by item it is starker — the median item has a standard deviation of 0.186 across all 594 personas against 1.013 for the real answers, and 9 of the 33 items have a standard deviation below 0.05, meaning the model returns effectively the same answer no matter whose name you put in the prompt.

Rank of the true politician. A cleverer test, designed to survive exactly the failure mode that sank MAE. Take the vector simulated for politician X. Measure its distance to all 594 real vectors and sort them. Where does the real X come in that ranking? Only the ordering matters, so it does not care that everything clusters near the midpoint — it only asks whether X's simulation leans toward the real X relative to everyone else.

Chance is the 50th percentile. Measured: median 52.1%, against a floor of 50.1% established by constant vectors. Top-1 accuracy is 0.002, which is exactly 1/594 — one lucky hit. The model cannot identify which politician it is simulating.

A design error caught by a known-answer fixture, then corrected again The first version of this metric was checked on a small fixture, which reported that a constant vector scores the 68.4th percentile — well above chance — and a plausible-sounding explanation was written down: a constant vector sits near the population centroid, so it is closer than average to everyone. That explanation was wrong. On the full sweep the constants land at exactly 50.1%, as they must: a name-independent vector induces one fixed ordering of the 594 candidates, so as the persona ranges over the whole set its rank takes every value exactly once. The 68.4% was sampling noise from a 60-persona fixture. A number that confirms a story you find satisfying still needs the sanity check that would refute it.

The skew that explains both. Simulated answers have a mean of 2.271; real answers average 2.763. Every persona leans "agree", regardless of who it is. The three targets make this concrete:

Step 5 · Where in the chain does it break?

At this point a natural proposal appears: if the personas can't answer surveys, why not train them on survey answers? Before spending a training run on that, there is a control that settles it, and it is the strongest test available anywhere in this project.

The four anchors have survey answers of their own — they are politicians too. And unlike the targets, they were fine-tuned on 12,000–20,000 of their own speeches, not 30. So administering the survey to each anchor's own adapter is the maximum-resource case: their real answers, their own fully-trained adapter, no merge, no blending, no low-resource approximation of anything. What this rules out The failure is not in the merge, and not in the low-resource approximation. It is in the step before both: a model fine-tuned on twenty thousand of a politician's own speeches still cannot answer that politician's survey. Speech style and stated policy positions are not connected by this pipeline at any resource level. Improving the merge could not have fixed it, and neither can more target data.
Decision: do not train the anchors on survey answers This is the tempting fix and it has to be refused, for a reason worth understanding precisely. It would not be label leakage — the anchors' answers are not the targets' answers, and no target's ground truth would ever be seen. But the 33 items are identical across politicians, so training on them teaches the task format, the meaning of the 1–5 scale, and the population's overall answer distribution. The claim would quietly degrade from "personas fitted to speech encode ideology that an independent instrument can read" to "models trained on surveys can answer surveys", and the merge would then transport survey-answering competence — masking the very distinction this whole session was built to establish. It is legitimate only as a labelled diagnostic, an upper bound on whether the merge can transport ideology at all. Never as evidence for the method.
Decision: stop chasing absolute survey accuracy Two scorers, a 594-person population control, a rank metric, a measured constant floor and a full-resource anchor self-test all agree that this protocol does not carry politician identity at this scale — with or without the merge, and with or without abundant data. The correct move is not a third scorer. The paper reports the survey result as a limitation with the diagnostic attached, and the headline rests on NLL.
The closure was re-opened once, on principle, and it held This section was closed on the evidence that these personas carry no identity at all. §19 then showed that premise to be objective-dependent — SFT anchors do carry recoverable party signal — which meant the closure rested on something that had turned out not to be true in general. So the whole instrument was re-administered on the new anchors, at 33 targets, at a cost of two hours of GPU.

The null survived. That is what makes this a result rather than an abandoned thread, and it puts a boundary on §19: SFT anchors carry recoverable party signal in speech-likelihood space and carry none of it into stated survey positions. Re-running a closed negative result after its premise changes is unglamorous and it is what turns "we gave up on that" into "we know where the effect stops."

The finding that survives all of this

NLL and the survey metric are anti-correlated. The uniform merge has by far the worst NLL — 2.5558 on SFT anchors, 2.7689 on DPO anchors, against a base of 2.3613 — yet posts the best or near-best survey score on some targets, and the tuned merge, which wins on NLL every time, is never the best survey configuration.

Optimising how well a model predicts someone's speech does not transfer to getting their stated policy positions right. Those are different targets, and improving one can cost you the other. Because this now survives the objective fix, it is a statement about what transfers rather than an artefact of badly trained anchors — which is a much stronger thing to be able to say, and it is the difference between a limitation and an excuse.

A rejected fix, and the reason it's instructive

One proposal kept resurfacing: politicians answer 3 ("no strong view") on policies they don't care about, so the midpoint dummy is winning on items where nobody has a position. Drop those and evaluate only where the politician took a real stance.

The premise is entirely correct. Across all 594×33 answers, 31.7% are 3 — it is the single most common response. 64 members answer 3 to more than half the items, one to every single item. So the objection is real.

The conclusion still doesn't follow, and the test that shows why is worth internalising. Each filter below recomputes the constant baseline on the same filtered set, so the comparison stays fair:

There is also a methodological line here worth naming. Dropping items the whole population answers 3 to is defensible — it is a property of the instrument, applied identically to every politician and every method, and removing non-discriminating items is standard. Dropping answers this particular politician gave as 3 is different: it selects the evaluation set using the label you are trying to predict. And the substantive objection is stronger than the statistical one — knowing when a politician abstains is part of simulating them, so removing those items deletes the more politically interesting half of the task. The fence-sitting rate is a finding worth reporting, not a filter.

The reframing: the instrument works, the knowledge doesn't

Now the reversal. The natural reading of everything above is that survey-answering is the wrong format for a model trained on speech — the training prompt is a name, a party, 500 characters of committee exchange and a free-form reply, while the survey is a bare proposition and one token from {1,2,3,4,5}. Those really are very different tasks.

But format is testable, and it is not the bottleneck. Under exactly that prompt and exactly that scoring, with the base model and no adapter at all:

Two more rejected proposals, briefly

Tune the optimiser on some survey items and test on the rest. Rejected, and the reason is bracing: a nearest-neighbour lookup — find the most similar politician among the other 593 on the 16 tuning items, copy their answers for the held-out 17 — scores MAE 0.647, against 0.824 for the best constant and 1.137 for the 7B model, beating the constant for 396 of 594 politicians. The task is solved without a language model, speech data, adapters or a merge. It also presumes you have the target's survey answers, which is exactly what the low-resource setting says you don't.

Train the anchors on survey data. Rejected for now, though the leakage question is cleaner than it looks: the two waves share 29 of 33 item codes but zero questions have identical text — the codes are positional — so cross-wave training would genuinely hold the questions out. It fails on other grounds. No anchor appears in both waves, so at most half of them could be trained; and it targets format adaptation, which 岸田's 96th percentile already shows is not what is failing.

The queue, in the project's own order

1 · Sixty trials at twelve dimensions

~25 minutes of GPU on one target. The granularity result is currently defensible only at equal trial budget; this separates "per-depth structure does not help" from "12 dims is under-optimised at 20 trials". Cheap, and the only thing a reviewer is likely to press on.

2 · The anchor-count slope, from adapters already on disk

Nested CV at 2, 3 and 4 anchors with the subset rotated across targets, so the curve is not tied to one combination. Needs no new training — --adapters is already explicit. If 3→4 is flat, extrapolating to 20 is fantasy and four training runs are saved; if it is still climbing, eight anchors becomes the obvious next build and the VRAM work in §23 is justified. Better motivated than it was, since the argument against it just dissolved.

3 · The budget sweep

~4 h. Does the ordering reverse at 4–8 instances? Nested-subset budgets are built so every point on the curve pairs with every other. Weaker motivation than when it was proposed — it existed as "the last place a positive claim could live", and the claim is no longer in danger at 24 — but it is the most direct extension of the low-resource thesis, and §15's characters-per-utterance finding suggests the x-axis should be characters.

4 · The one that would change the paper: different-politician negatives

§22 argues this at length. Briefly: the DPO negative is real X versus an LLM playing X, so both sides argue X's positions and the only gradient available pushes on authenticity of register — which is exactly what §18 measures the adapters as having learned. A pair construction carrying positional signal exists in the same corpus at zero extra cost: real X versus a real utterance by an opposing-party politician on comparable context. That changes the preference direction from "human, not LLM" to "X's stance, not Y's stance". It is the most promising untried idea here.

The general shape of this The interesting work in this project was not the method — that was mostly assembling known pieces. It was the instrumentation: adding a dumb baseline, running a 594-person control, inventing a rank metric that survives the failure mode that killed MAE, testing the chain at maximum resource before trying to improve it at minimum, re-running old targets to prove the environment hadn't drifted, running the one baseline most likely to make the whole method redundant, and re-opening a closed negative result when its premise turned out to be conditional. Notice how many of those are attempts to break a result rather than to strengthen one.

The pattern is worth stating plainly, because it repeated three times. A baseline nearly killed the method; stacking it rescued the method; the control on that rescue showed the contribution was five times smaller than the headline; and then a one-hour probe showed that every one of those measurements had been conditioned on an invisible choice. Each step was someone trying to falsify the previous one. A result you cannot get a null out of is a result you cannot trust — and the most useful entries in the decision log are the ones where the answer changed.

22 · An assessment: what is fragile, and how to make this research robust

Everything before this point reports the project's own artifacts and decisions. This section changes voice: it is an outside review — what this research does well, where it is genuinely vulnerable, and what would harden it. Disagreement with some of it is expected; that is what the section is for.

Credit where due

The process here is better than most published work in this area. Constant baselines before interpretation; a control arm (best-single+ICL) on every rescue; re-running old targets to prove the environment didn't drift; a decision log that records wrong turns instead of erasing them; fixed transferred hyperparameters for the rival method instead of a strawman; re-opening a closed negative result once its premise turned out to be conditional. The reason the project keeps producing painful results is that it keeps building instruments capable of producing them. That is the right failure mode.

Two things deserve specific credit because they are unusual. First, when the anchor objective was implicated, the response was a one-anchor probe rather than four training runs on a hunch — an hour of GPU that overturned a headline result. Second, the paper is generated from paper_numbers.py, a single stdlib script that re-derives every quoted figure from committed artifacts. Nothing in it is transcribed from a log line, and running it is how two errors in the decision log were caught (a mis-stated p-value, and a "second independent selection rule" that was the same rule twice).

The load-bearing code paths behind §15–§19 were reviewed for this assessment: fold pairing (the baselines import fold_indices from the merge script rather than reimplementing it, and neither side exposes a seed to drift on), ICL prefix hygiene (dev-fold only, scored at base weights, one shared builder), the continuation-only NLL slice, the per-fold adapter-removal guard, and the speechID join on DPO negatives. One suspected silent-drop bug — instances whose prompt fills the 1024-token training cap — was chased down with the real tokenizer and does not occur (target prompts top out at 851 tokens). The comparisons in §15–§17 are implemented the way they are described.

Design weaknesses

1 · The cheapest killer baselines ran last

ICL costs nothing to implement. SFT is 11 seconds a fold. Both were in the spec from day one as required baselines — one labelled the kill criterion — and both ran months after the method was built, tuned, scaled and written up. Each rewrote the paper's conclusion within a day of running, and the fourth baseline (DPO-on-target), which looked like the most redundant of the seven, is the one that exposed the confound behind both. The general rule: run the baseline most likely to kill the method before investing in the method — and run all of them, because the informative one is not identifiable in advance.

2 · A fixed upstream choice was a hidden confound for the entire study

This is now demonstrated rather than suspected, so it is worth restating at full strength. The anchor training objective was chosen once, early, never varied, and every downstream comparison silently conditioned on it — the ICL tie, the 0/33 fine-tuning defeat, the granularity null, the party inversion, and the first reading of the survey null. Four of those five reversed or changed meaning when it was varied. Any parameter that expensive to change deserves a one-unit probe before commitment. It is also worth asking, of any pipeline, which choice here has never been varied? — and treating the answer as a live confound rather than a settled fact.

3 · The DPO negative can only teach authenticity, not position — and this is now measured

The training pairs are real X vs an LLM role-playing X (§4). Both sides are X-flavoured and both argue X's positions; what differs is authenticity of register. So the only gradient available pushes on register — and §18 measures the adapters as having learned overwhelmingly register, with §19 showing the ideological residue surviving only under the objective that doesn't use these negatives. The paper's own appendix example makes the point concretely: the caricature is fluent, on-topic and correctly partisan, and what it lacks is procedural specificity, not a position.

A pair construction carrying positional signal exists in the same corpus at zero extra cost: real X vs a real utterance by a different politician on comparable context, ideally opposing-party. The preference direction becomes "X's stance, not Y's stance" rather than "human, not LLM". This is the single most promising untried idea in the project, and unlike most of the queue it could plausibly move the survey result rather than just the likelihood.

4 · The objective metric mostly cannot see the thing being claimed

Held-out NLL over full utterances is dominated by function words, honorifics and parliamentary boilerplate; the tokens that encode a policy position are a small minority. §18 quantifies exactly this — three quarters of the measurable effect is genre. The qualification the earlier version of this assessment could not make is that the metric is not blind: §19 extracts a party signal at p = 0.0013 from the same likelihoods, so there is ideology in there. It is just outnumbered four to one. Options remain: score NLL only on content-bearing spans; evaluate on stance-labelled statements; or move to a behavioural instrument such as roll-call votes, which give many labelled decisions per politician and put the bill text in the prompt, so the metric is not gated on the model's prior knowledge of the person.

5 · Unquestioned constants: the budget and the persona prompt

The 30-utterance budget was never derived — the log says so — and the sweep is still unrun. The evaluation persona is conditioned on the bare name while training prompts carry name plus party in a different format; nobody has ablated that. Both are cheap (name+party at eval; budget at 4/8/16) and both would pin down how much of a result is an artefact of a constant. Add a third to the list on the strength of §15's appendix finding: utterances are the wrong unit for a low-resource budget when median utterance length varies 1.7× across the resource range.

Statistical weaknesses

6 · "Best configuration in the study" is still a post-hoc selection

Eleven configurations are ranked on the same 165 folds and the winner is then described as best. This is better than it was — BO+ICL beats the runner-up SFT+ICL on 27/33 with p = 3.2×10⁻⁴, so the top of the table is now a paired result rather than a decimal-place comparison. But the fine ordering below it is not: several adjacent rows differ by less than their paired resolution, and the paper is right to rank only where the sign test is decisive and to mark the rest as ties.

7 · One draw of 33 targets is carrying many analyses

The 33-target sample was drawn once, and every subsequent claim — prominence bands, self-similarity correlations, the fine-tuning verdict, the affinity test, the coefficient post-mortems, the survey re-point — is computed on it. Each analysis is fine alone; together they are a garden of forking paths on a single sample. The fix is unusually cheap here: 517 politicians remain eligible, so the headline claims can be re-confirmed on a fresh 30-target draw for about five GPU-hours. Designate the current sample exploratory and the fresh draw confirmatory, and decide the confirmatory claims before running it. This is now the highest-value statistical work available, precisely because the claims have stopped moving.

8 · Single seed, and 33 targets that share four anchors

The fold split, the optimiser and the DARE masks all run at one fixed seed; headline numbers should be shown stable across two or three. And the sign tests treat the 33 targets as independent draws, but they share the same four anchors and the same base model. For anchor-specific conclusions this matters most: the affinity test's 28 observations are 28 targets against four anchors, so the effective sample for "SFT anchors carry party" is smaller than 28 and p = 0.0013 is optimistic. The permutation null is the right instrument and the direction is well supported; the decimal places are not.

The instruments are underpowered for the question being asked

One analysis, run for this walkthrough on the committed 594-persona dump rather than by the project, reframes the survey chapter. The rank metric and MAE both read "no identity signal" — but they are low-power instruments, and a more sensitive one finds signal they cannot see:

  • Per-item correlation between simulated and real answers across all 594 politicians: mean r = +0.050, 23/33 items positive, permutation p ≈ 0.005.
  • Projected onto the survey's main ideological axis (the first principal component, 33.5% of real answer variance): r = +0.20.
  • Within party only — removing everything the model could get by knowing the party — r = +0.095, positive in all four parties with n ≥ 10.

Weak, but real, and partly individual rather than party-level. The rank-among-594 metric needs roughly r ≈ 0.7 before it moves off 50%, so at r ≈ 0.2 it reads chance no matter what an intervention does — it cannot register progress. A population-level correlation at n = 594 has a standard error near 0.04 and detects a change of 0.1. Steer by the sensitive instrument; keep the rank metric as the honest headline check. "No identity" should be read as "identity signal of r ≈ 0.2, invisible to the metrics used so far" — a different claim, with different next steps.

§19 is the same lesson learned independently inside the project, in the other measurement space. A permutation test on which anchor a target prefers found ideology at p = 0.0013 in exactly the likelihoods where the headline comparisons had shown nothing but register. When a metric reads "no signal", the first question is whether it could have read anything else.

The path forward, ranked

  1. Rebuild the negatives, not the method — real-opposing-politician completions instead of LLM caricatures (point 3). It is the only queued change that targets the thing every measurement says is missing, and it costs one export plus four training runs.
  2. Pre-commit and run a confirmatory 30-target draw (point 7). Five GPU hours converts a well-instrumented exploratory study into a confirmed one, and the claims are finally stable enough to pre-commit.
  3. Re-power the external metric before re-running it — population correlation as the steering instrument, and evaluate roll-call votes as a behavioural ground truth with real statistical depth per politician.
  4. Close the two cheap open ablations — 60 trials at 12 dims, and the anchor-count slope from adapters already on disk. Both are under half an hour of GPU and both currently sit as caveats in the paper.
  5. Seed stability on the headline rows (point 8). Two extra seeds on three targets is enough to say the numbers do not depend on one draw of a DARE mask.
What the paper ended up being, and whether that was the right call It would have been defensible to write this as a pure negative result: task-vector persona composition at 7B transports parliamentary register, not individual identity. For two days that was the honest reading, and the decision log says explicitly that the choice should be made deliberately rather than by omission.

The objective finding is what made a different paper possible, and the shape the paper chose — leading the contributions with the register decomposition rather than the main table, then the recoverable ideology, then the objective — is an editorial judgement worth noticing. It puts the largest effect first even though the largest effect is the least flattering one, and it means a reader meets "three quarters of this is genre" before meeting "and our method wins". This assessment's view is that this is the correct ordering, and that the negative result on the survey instrument is worth as much as either. Negative results with this level of instrumentation are rarer and more useful than the paper originally planned to be.

23 · Engineering traps worth more than they look

The silent no-op

A 7B model in bf16 needs ~15 GB. On an 8 GB GPU, device_map="auto" helpfully splits it, leaving overflow layers as meta tensors — placeholders that carry shape but no storage, materialised on demand during a forward pass.

Forward passes work fine. But the merge writes weights in place, and an in-place write to a meta tensor is silently discarded. The result: merges that appear to succeed, coefficients for the upper layers that do nothing, and an optimiser cheerfully searching a partly-flat objective for hours. Even the reversibility check passed — a no-op write reverses to a no-op.

The fix is to refuse to run rather than to run wrongly:

meta = [p for p in self.modules
        if self._params[p + ".weight"].device.type == "meta"]
if meta:
    raise RuntimeError(
        f"{len(meta)} of {len(self.modules)} target modules are meta tensors "
        f"(offloaded) ... In-place merge writes would silently no-op.")

research/polis/merge_layer_group.py:98–106

Generalisable lesson The dangerous bugs in ML systems are rarely crashes — they are the ones that return plausible numbers. When an operation can be silently skipped, assert that it happened.

This one is worth generalising past the specific bug, and the paper does so in its discussion: a flat objective is not distinguishable from a difficult one by looking at the optimisation trace. The optimiser converges either way. So every run here is gated behind a self-test requiring four distinct likelihoods under four coefficient settings plus exact restoration of the base value — and any weight-space merging study should adopt something equivalent, because the failure mode is not specific to meta tensors.

Memory that scales the wrong way

The merger keeps every anchor's reconstructed ΔW resident as a dense fp32 tensor. That is cheap at 0.5B and becomes the dominant cost quickly, because ΔW has the shape of the full weight matrix even though it came from a rank-16 factorisation:

Model weights (bf16) Dense fp32 anchor deltas — the part that scales badly

Dense fp32 deltas for 4 anchors, plus the bf16 model and its snapshots. At 32B the deltas alone exceed the model itself.

The fix follows from a detail of apply(): it already iterates module by module, so all the deltas never need to exist at once. Keep only the small LoRA factors and rebuild each module's ΔW inside the loop — recomputing B·A costs milliseconds on a GPU and drops 32B from ~129 GB to ~69 GB, which is the difference between four cards and one.

What actually went wrong on the day

The first 7B session — the three-target run whose paired folds §15 plots — was a rented GPU on a two-hour clock, which is a good stress test of how much of a research pipeline is real. Roughly the first hour went to these four:

SymptomCause, and what it teaches
FileNotFoundError on a data file, in one script out of three That script's --dpo-dir defaulted to the full corpus, which lives on a laptop SSD and was never copied to the rented box. The other two scripts were fine because the shell runner set the path explicitly. A default that works on your machine is a landmine on someone else's. The same footgun bit a second script a day later, in the same way, after the model had already spent minutes loading — both defaults have now been changed to the subset that actually ships.
Out of memory on a card that had just run the same model fine The script built a throwaway merger object purely to read n_layers off it, never freed it, then built another. Each one pins ~14.8 GB. Two of those plus a 15.2 GB model overruns 48 GB. Reading the layer count off the adapter file instead dropped peak usage to ~30 GB. In GPU code, "I just need one field from it" still allocates the whole object.
The runbook and the pasted commands disagreed about --device An uncommitted edit removing --device cuda had been sitting in the working tree and got committed without anyone reading the diff. Review your own diff before committing, especially the parts you did not write today.
Ran the wrong 52-minute job instead of the right 12-minute one The instruction was "the snippet below section 6". There were fifteen runnable blocks and no way to name one unambiguously. They all have IDs now. If a runbook has more than a handful of commands, they need names.
Adding one row to a results table cost a 7-hour re-run The 33-target run kept each fold's fitted merge in memory only. When the next experiment needed one more configuration scored against those same merges, none of them existed any more — so getting two extra evaluation passes meant re-deriving every Bayesian optimisation from scratch. There is now a --dump-picks flag and a standing rule to set it. If a computation was expensive, persist its output, not just the summary you happened to want at the time.
Output written to the filesystem root, three separate times Several runbook blocks set a shell variable on their first line and use it below. Paste a block starting from line two and the variable is unset — and an unset variable in bash expands to nothing, so "$ART/run.log" silently becomes /run.log. The error you eventually see is "no such file", which sends you hunting for a missing file rather than a missing variable. Prefer literal paths in a runbook, or set -u so an unset variable is an error rather than an empty string.
Two hours of rented GPU spent running the wrong protocol on all 33 targets The command was one line of environment-variable prefixes: ADAPTERS=… NESTED=1 ICL=1 DUMP_PICKS=… TARGETS=… ./run_7b_bo_gpu.sh. The terminal wrapped it, the shell received only the tail from TARGETS= onward, and the runner silently fell back to its defaults — single-split, wrong anchors, survey evaluation on — for every target. It even looked like good news: single-split does one optimisation per target instead of five, so it presented as a "3× speedup".

This was the seventh instance of the same failure in this workflow, which is the point. It was finally fixed structurally rather than by resolving to be careful: there are now purpose-built scripts that take nothing on the command line, verify every adapter exists before loading a 7B model, and rename their logs afterwards so a later run cannot append into them. If a mistake has happened seven times, the mistake is in the interface, not in the operator.

A clock that lies, and logs that append

Two smaller ones from the same sessions, both of which cost time reading results rather than producing them. The rented pod's clock ran about nine hours behind local time, so log timestamps for an evening session read as morning — harmless until you are trying to work out which of two runs came first. And the batch runner appends, so an aborted attempt sits above the good block in the same file; the summariser was taught to read only the last complete block, which is the right fix, but anyone reading the raw log by eye will find the failed run first.

24 · Run it yourself

Everything above is runnable. All the code lives in one directory, research/polis/, and the whole pipeline works at 0.5B scale on a laptop — which is exactly why the development study is at 0.5B.

The one-minute version

Each module has a self-test that needs no data beyond the adapters. Start here, because it proves the merge is real: 

# does the merge apply, reverse exactly, and respond to per-group coefficients?
python merge_layer_group.py --device cuda

# are the anchors actually pointing in different directions?
python anchor_delta_cosine.py

# can the model answer a Likert item at all?
python polis_option_logprob.py --demo
Read the self-test output, don't just check it exits 0 merge_layer_group.py prints four NLLs — base, uniform, upper-group-only, lower-group-only — plus a restored base. They must be four distinct numbers and the restore must match base exactly. If they collapse, the merge is silently no-oping (§23) and every hour after that is wasted. Printing [OK] is not sufficient; that line is reached either way.

The full pipeline, in order

# 1. pick anchors (writes data/data/polis/anchors.json)
python select_polis_anchors.py --max-anchors 4

# 2. extract prompt/chosen pairs for one anchor
python export_polis_dpo_pairs.py --person-id 152 --k 4 --max-pairs 1000

# 3. fill in the `rejected` caricature -- DPO arm only, needs a Gemini API key
python generate_polis_rejected.py --person-id 152

# 4a. train that anchor's adapter with DPO (uses prompt/chosen/rejected)
python train_one_politician_persona.py \
    --data ../../data/data/polis/dpo_pairs_full/152.jsonl \
    --output output/polis_152_kishida --epochs 1

# 4b. ... or with SFT (same file, `rejected` ignored). This is the arm the
#     paper's main table uses, and the difference is the whole of §19.
python train_anchor_sft.py --person-id 152 \
    --output output/polis_152_sft_qwen7b

# 5. ... repeat 2-4 for 2377, 3631, 5520, then optimise the merge
python bo_merge_coeffs.py --target 1279 --budget 30 --trials 40 \
    --dpo-dir ../../data/data/polis/dpo_pairs_targets

Step 5 is the experiment. Add --nested for the unbiased cross-validated version (§13), or --utas-eval with a wave file to get the external survey metric (§14) alongside the NLL — and if you do, add --utas-numeric, because the default scorer is the one §14 explains is broken.

The population run (§15 and §16)

The 33-target batch is driven through one shell runner. The general-purpose entry point takes everything by environment variable — ICL=1 adds the prompting baseline of §16, and DUMP_PICKS saves each fold's fitted coefficients so the next question doesn't cost another seven hours of re-derivation: 

# ~10 min per target; run it inside tmux, the SSH session will drop
NESTED=1 ICL=1 \
  DUMP_PICKS=specs/polis-low-resource-persona/artifacts/bo7b_picks \
  TARGETS="1279 2053 2289 369 2447 1572 688 ..." \
  ./research/polis/scripts/run_7b_bo_gpu.sh

# pool 33 logs into the paper's table -- stdlib only, no GPU, seconds
python nested_log_summary.py \
    --logs ../../specs/polis-low-resource-persona/artifacts/bo7b_gpu_logs \
    --glob 'n33_target_*.log'

The --glob is not optional — the artifacts directory holds five protocols side by side and the summariser refuses to pool across them. It also reads only the last complete block in each file, so aborted attempts sitting above the good results don't need cleaning up first.

Prefer the fixed scripts to the flexible one That command is exactly the shape that cost two hours of rented GPU when a terminal wrapped it (§23). The runs behind the paper's tables use single-purpose scripts that take no arguments at all — run_sft33.sh, run_granularity_sft.sh, run_utas_sft.sh — each of which verifies every adapter exists before loading a 7B model and renames its log afterwards so a later run cannot append into it. Nothing gets pasted but a script name.

The baselines that decided whether any of this was necessary

Fine-tuning directly on the target's own sentences is the comparison the method lost in §17. Both variants live in one script, and --icl scores each trained adapter with the same prefix the merge is scored under, so the two paths cannot drift apart: 

python sparse_target_baselines.py --target 1279 --icl \
    --objective sft            # spec baseline 3; --objective dpo is baseline 4
    --base Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct --device cuda

One detail in there is a fairness decision, not a default: the +ICL row is reported at the hyperparameter cell chosen on the prefix-free score. Letting each row pick its own best cell would give the fine-tuning baseline a second look at the held-out fold, and a small grid search is already optimistic enough.

The diagnostics from §20

These are the two scripts that turned an uninterpretable survey table into a conclusion, and they are the cheapest things in the repository. Neither trains anything; the first needs a GPU for ten minutes, the second runs on a laptop in seconds.
