POLIS: composing personas in weight space

How do you simulate a politician who has barely said anything on the record? This is a guided tour of the actual code behind the POLIS paper — every concept paired with the lines that implement it, and every number taken from the project's own data files. If you have taken one machine-learning course and know roughly what a neural network and a language model are, §1 will get you the rest of the way.

How to read this §1–§14 build the method up from scratch; every unfamiliar term gets defined where it first appears, and there is a glossary at the end of §1. §15–§17 are the results, and they are genuinely mixed: one measurement says the method works, a second says nothing at all, and most of the interesting work went into figuring out which of those two conclusions to believe. If you only read three sections, read §1, §15, and §16.

1 · Primer: five ideas you need first

Everything in this paper follows from five ideas. None of them require more than first-year linear algebra. If they are already familiar, skip to §2.

① A model is a big pile of numbers

Qwen2.5-7B — the model used here — is about 7.6 billion numbers, organised into matrices. When you feed it text, it multiplies that text (as vectors) through those matrices and produces a probability for every possible next word. The numbers are called weights, and they are the entire model. Change them and you change what it says.

② Fine-tuning means nudging those numbers

To make a model imitate a specific person, you show it that person's text and adjust the weights so their sentences become more likely. The model does not gain a "Kishida module" — every relevant weight simply shifts a little. Write the finished weights as Wafter = Wbefore + ΔW. That ΔW ("delta W", the change) is the entire product of fine-tuning, and it is a matrix of the same shape as the original.

③ ΔW is a vector, so you can do arithmetic on it

This is the leap. If you flatten a ΔW into one long list of numbers, it is a vector — a point, or a direction, in a space with hundreds of millions of dimensions. And vectors can be added, scaled, and averaged.

So 0.5 × ΔWKishida + 0.5 × ΔWShiokawa is a perfectly well-defined thing to compute. Whether it means "halfway between those two politicians" is an empirical question — and it is the question the whole paper is testing. This idea is usually called a task vector.

④ "Weight space" is where those vectors live

Picture the base model sitting at the origin. Each fine-tuned politician is a point some distance away, in their own direction. Blend those directions and you land somewhere in between — and the set of all reachable blends is the span of your anchors.

Schematic — the real space has ~822 million dimensions, and no honest 2-D picture of it exists. What is real is the consequence: a target inside the shaded region can be reached by blending; one outside it cannot. §3 measures how often that actually happens.

⑤ Log-likelihood: scoring a guess

Finally we need a way to say whether one persona is better than another. A language model assigns a probability to every next word, so given a sentence a real politician actually said, you can ask: what probability did this model assign to those exact words?

Probabilities of long sentences are astronomically small, so we take logarithms and add them instead of multiplying — and then flip the sign so that smaller is better. That gives negative log-likelihood (NLL), measured in nats per word.

Low NLL = the model found the real sentence unsurprising = it is a good imitation. This is the score POLIS optimises, and it appears in every results table.

The whole paper in one sentence Fine-tune four data-rich politicians, treat their ΔW as vectors, and for a politician with almost no data, search for the blend of those four vectors that makes their handful of real sentences least surprising.

2 · The problem: you cannot fine-tune on data you don't have

"Silicon sampling" is the idea of using an LLM to stand in for a human respondent — ask the model to role-play a person and record its answers as if they were survey data. The failure mode is immediate and well-documented: ask a model to be a politician and it produces a caricature assembled from media coverage, not that person's actual positions.

Fine-tuning on the person's own words fixes this — if the words exist. In the Japanese Diet, a handful of party leaders have tens of thousands of transcribed utterances, while most backbenchers have almost none. That long tail is exactly who a representative simulation needs, and exactly where per-person fine-tuning is impossible.

The reframe: locate, don't train

POLIS treats ideology as a continuous space and the weight-changes from fine-tuning as composable vectors in that space. Train adapters only on the few data-rich politicians ("anchors"). For a sparse target, don't train anything — find the blend of anchors that best explains the handful of sentences the target did say. The target's data is used to locate a point, not to fit a model. With 4 anchors and 3 depth groups that's 12 numbers to determine, which is a very different statistical problem from fine-tuning billions of parameters on 30 sentences.

3 · Choosing the anchors — and the limit nobody can optimise away

Before any training happens, someone has to decide which four politicians become the anchors. This choice is more consequential than it looks: a blend of anchors can only ever express positions that lie between them. Pick four politicians who all agree with each other and there is nothing to interpolate.

The selection is automated. Every politician gets a speech count from their archive, and an ideological position from the project's existing scaling data — a file with 55 separate policy "axes" (immigration, defence, nuclear power, …), each placing politicians on a 1-D line.

4 · Building the data: session-grounded preference pairs

Before any modelling, we need training examples that capture how a specific politician actually talks. The trick here is session grounding: rather than isolated quotes, each example reconstructs the real conversational context from the Diet transcript.

For every real utterance by an anchor, one training instance is emitted:

FieldContent
promptThe last K speeches before this utterance in the same sitting, plus a cue for the anchor to speak
chosenWhat the politician actually said next
rejectedA strong LLM (Gemini 2.5-flash-lite) role-playing that same politician on the same context
Why this matters The rejected side is the paper's argument made literal. The model isn't just learning "sound like a politician" — it's learning "the real person, not the LLM's stereotype of them." The thing the paper criticises is the exact negative the training signal pushes away from.

The immediately preceding speech is always kept intact even when truncating, because it is usually the question being answered — drop it and the reply becomes incoherent.

Read those two side by side and the paper's thesis stops being abstract. The caricature is not wrong — it is plausible, on-topic, and correctly partisan. It is wrong in a subtler way: it performs a party identity instead of doing a job. Training on "prefer the left over the right" is training away from exactly that failure.

A choice the code documents against itself The paper's spec called for the caricature to come from the model being fine-tuned. The implementation uses Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite instead — cheaper, no GPU — and the source file says so in its own docstring rather than quietly diverging: "the DPO signal becomes 'prefer the real utterance over a capable general LLM's role-play' rather than 'over the policy's own.' Recorded so the choice is explicit." (generate_polis_rejected.py:10–17) Worth noticing as a habit — a deviation written down is a deviation a reviewer can evaluate.

Context is capped at ~1200 characters as a proxy for the trainer's 1024-token limit (Japanese runs roughly one token per character). In practice the resulting prompts are ~1,336 tokens at the median — a number that comes back to haunt the compute budget later.

5 · DPO: learning from pairs without a reward model

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is how each anchor adapter is trained. The setup: you have pairs of responses where one is better than the other, and you want the model to prefer the better one. The classic recipe for this is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), which does it in two stages — first train a separate "reward model" to score responses, then use reinforcement learning to push the language model toward high-scoring output. DPO's contribution is a piece of algebra showing you can skip the middle step entirely: the two stages collapse into one loss function you can minimise with ordinary gradient descent.

L = − log σ( β · [ log πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] )

Reading it piece by piece:

  • πθ is the model being trained; πref is a frozen copy of where it started.
  • yw ("win") is the real utterance; yl ("lose") is the caricature.
  • Each ratio asks: has this response become more likely than it was before training? Dividing by the reference is what keeps the model from wandering off — it can only be rewarded for moving relatively.
  • The difference of the two ratios is a score: high when the model has raised the real utterance and lowered the caricature. σ is the sigmoid, the S-shaped function that squashes any number into the range (0, 1) — so the score becomes a probability, and taking −log of it makes it a loss that is small when the probability is near 1.
  • β ("beta") is a dial: small β lets the model drift far from where it started, large β keeps it close. Set to 0.1 here.

Two diagnostics get reported during training, and both appear in the project logs: rewards/accuracies (how often the real utterance scores above the caricature — reached 1.0) and rewards/margins (by how much — reached 7–9). The four 7B anchors all trained cleanly to these values.

The training configuration is small enough to read in full — and worth reading, because every number in it is a compute-budget decision:

A batch size of one is not a typo. DPO holds two models in memory (the policy and its frozen reference) and processes two completions per example, so the memory cost per step is roughly four times ordinary fine-tuning. Accumulating gradients over 8 steps recovers an effective batch of 8 without ever holding 8 examples at once. This is the standard toolkit for training a 7B model on hardware that has no business training one.

Careful — a distinction that comes back in §15 High margins mean the adapter reliably separates real from fake. That is not the same as having learned ideology. At 0.5B the adapters largely captured register — the cadence and vocabulary of Diet speech, independent of what is being argued — which is why one anchor kept winning as a generic "fluent-parliamentarian" backbone regardless of the target's party. Learning how a politician talks and learning what a politician believes are separable, and almost every finding in §15–§17 turns on which of the two the method actually captured.

6 · LoRA and the idea of a "task vector"

Fine-tuning every weight of a 7B model per politician is unaffordable. LoRA freezes the base model and learns a low-rank correction instead. For a weight matrix W ∈ ℝout×in, it learns two thin matrices A ∈ ℝr×in and B ∈ ℝout×r with r = 16, and the effective weight becomes W + ΔW where:

ΔW = (α / r) · B · A

The trick is one of shape. Multiplying an out×r matrix by an r×in matrix gives an out×in result — the same shape as W — but you only ever stored the two thin factors. Rank r is the width of the bottleneck: everything the adapter learned has to squeeze through 16 dimensions. α/r is a fixed scaling constant from the config (α=32, r=16, so the scale is 2).

At the shipped r=16 that is 10.1 million stored numbers standing in for 822 million of full-rank change — an 81× compression. On disk adapter_model.safetensors is 20 MB, versus ~15 GB for a full copy of the model; the 522 MB you actually have to ship to a GPU box is four of those plus their training checkpoints and tokenizer files. Note also that k_proj and v_proj are 512×3584 rather than 3584×3584: Qwen2.5 uses grouped-query attention, a memory-saving design where several attention heads share one set of keys and values, so those two projections are already narrow — which is why the 112 modules only come to 822M rather than the 1.4B you would get from four square projections per layer.

Here is the conceptual leap the whole paper rests on. Once training is done, ΔW is just a matrix — a direction in weight space that means "be more like Kishida." It can be added, scaled, and combined like any vector. This is the task vector view from §1③, and it is what makes "average two personas" a meaningful operation rather than a category error.

Note what the merge code does first, before anything else: it multiplies the factors back out. LoRA's compression is a storage convenience; the arithmetic happens in the full-size space. 

for pfx in prefixes:
    a = next(v for k, v in weights.items() if k.startswith(pfx) and ".lora_A" in k)
    b = next(v for k, v in weights.items() if k.startswith(pfx) and ".lora_B" in k)
    delta = (b.float() @ a.float()) * scaling            # [out, in]

That .float() is quietly expensive: it promotes each delta to fp32, which is where the 13 GB of resident anchor deltas in §18 comes from.

Does the "space" actually have room?

A blend of anchors can only express positions the anchors span. If all four ΔW pointed the same way, there would be nothing to interpolate — one shared direction with slightly different magnitudes. So the first thing the project checked was the pairwise cosine similarity between anchor task vectors (1.0 = identical direction, 0 = orthogonal).

Measured at 7B across 822,083,584 adapted parameters per anchor. The 0.9 threshold is the script's own kill switch — anchor_delta_cosine.py:96–106 prints [WARN] near-collinear anchor pairs and tells you to revisit the design. Everything here is far below it, so the anchors genuinely span a space.

The interesting part Look at the structure, not just the magnitudes. Every left-party pair (≈0.088) is more similar to each other than any of them is to the LDP anchor (≈0.064). At 0.5B these numbers were uniform regardless of party. Ideology is showing up as geometry — but note this is 3 pairs against 3 pairs, so it is suggestive, not a significance test.

Keep this in mind through §15 and §16, because it is the strongest pro-ideology evidence in the whole project and it does not survive contact with the downstream measurements. The anchor directions cluster by party; what gets built out of them does not behave like it knows anyone's party. Reconciling those two facts is unfinished business.

7 · DARE: drop most of the vector, keep the meaning

When you add several task vectors together they interfere — unrelated coordinates pile up into noise that degrades the model. DARE (Drop And REscale) is a surprisingly blunt fix: randomly delete ~90% of each task vector's entries, then multiply the survivors by 1/density to compensate.

keep with probability d · then multiply by 1/d

Why this doesn't destroy the adapter: the rescaling preserves the expected value of every entry (drop 90%, multiply the rest by 10, and the average is unchanged). Fine-tuning deltas turn out to be highly redundant, so a random 10% carries nearly the same information — while the collisions between different anchors become far rarer, since two sparse vectors rarely have non-zeros in the same place. 

mask = (torch.rand(delta.shape, generator=gen) < self.density)
delta = torch.where(mask, delta / self.density, torch.zeros_like(delta))

research/polis/merge_layer_group.py:84–85

Why generator=gen is load-bearing The mask is drawn from a seeded generator fixed once at construction. If the mask were redrawn on each merge, the same coefficients would give a different result every time — the optimiser in §11 would be chasing a moving target it cannot model. Freezing the randomness makes the objective a deterministic function of the coefficients. This is a general principle worth absorbing: stochastic components must be frozen before you optimise through them.

8 · TIES: settle the disagreements by vote

DARE reduces collisions but doesn't resolve them. When two anchors do both want to change the same weight in opposite directions, naive averaging cancels them into mush — a weight that means something to both anchors ends up meaning nothing.

TIES resolves this per-coordinate with an election. For each individual weight: sum the (already coefficient-weighted) contributions to decide a winning sign, discard every anchor that disagrees with that sign, and average only the survivors. A coordinate ends up decisively positive or decisively negative — never averaged into zero. 

def _ties(deltas):
    """TIES disjoint merge over already-weighted deltas [n_anchors, out, in]."""
    elected = torch.sign(deltas.sum(dim=0))                  # winning sign per weight
    agree   = (torch.sign(deltas) == elected) & (deltas != 0)  # who voted with it
    kept    = torch.where(agree, deltas, torch.zeros_like(deltas))
    count   = agree.sum(dim=0).clamp(min=1)
    return kept.sum(dim=0) / count                        # mean of the winners only

research/polis/merge_layer_group.py:146–153

Note clamp(min=1) — it guards the divide-by-zero when a coordinate is zero in every anchor (very common after a 90% drop). And the average is over agreeing anchors only, so a coordinate touched by one anchor keeps that anchor's full magnitude rather than being diluted by three zeros.

9 · Layer groups: the knob that makes this a search problem

So far a merge needs one weight per anchor. POLIS's contribution is that one number per anchor is not enough. Transformer layers specialise: earlier layers handle more lexical and syntactic structure, later layers more abstract and task-specific content. A persona is not uniformly distributed across depth — so the amount of each anchor you want to inject should vary by depth.

Layers are partitioned into G contiguous depth groups, and the coefficient becomes a matrix C ∈ ℝK×G — one value per (anchor, group) pair. With 4 anchors and 3 groups that is 12 free parameters, the entire "model" being fit to the sparse target. 

def _group_of(self, mod_path):
    layer = int(m.group(1)) if m else 0
    # contiguous depth partition: floor(layer / ceil(n_layers / n_groups))
    per = -(-self._n_layers // self.n_groups)  # ceil div
    return min(layer // per, self.n_groups - 1)

research/polis/merge_layer_group.py:117–122

Why custom code was needed The standard library (PEFT) offers add_weighted_adapter(combination_type="dare_ties"), but it only supports one global weight per adapter. The per-depth coefficient matrix is precisely what it cannot express, so the merge is reimplemented as direct tensor arithmetic. That is the reason merge_layer_group.py exists at all.

The merge, end to end

apply(C) is called once per optimiser trial. It restores the base weights, rebuilds the merge under the new coefficients, and writes the result in place — so a single loaded model is reused for hundreds of evaluations with no retraining and no reloading. 

for mod_path, slot in self.modules.items():
    base = self.base_weights[mod_path]
    stacked = []
    for ai, am in enumerate(slot):
        w = float(C[ai, am.group])          # ← coefficient for THIS anchor at THIS depth
        if w != 0.0:
            stacked.append(am.delta * w)
    merged = self._ties(torch.stack(stacked, dim=0))
    self._params[mod_path + ".weight"].data.copy_(
        (base.float() + merged.to(base.device)).to(base.dtype))

10 · The objective: how well does this persona predict real sentences?

To search over C we need a number that says how good a merge is. POLIS uses negative log-likelihood of the target's real utterances, given their real session context. In plain terms: show the merged model the room, and ask how surprised it is by what this person actually said. Lower is better. 

def mean_nll(self, instances):
    for prompt, cont in instances:
        ids_p = self.tok(prompt, ...).input_ids       # session context
        ids_c = self.tok(cont, ...).input_ids         # what they really said
        ids = torch.cat([ids_p, ids_c], dim=1)
        logits = self.model(ids).logits[:, :-1]
        logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)
        tok_logp = logp.gather(-1, ids[:, 1:].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)[0]
        n_ctx = ids_p.shape[1] - 1
        tot += -tok_logp[n_ctx:].mean().item()        # ← score ONLY the reply

research/polis/bo_merge_coeffs.py:74–87

Three details that are easy to skim past but matter:

  • logits[:, :-1] and ids[:, 1:] — the standard off-by-one of causal LMs. Position t's logits predict token t+1, so predictions and targets must be shifted against each other.
  • gather picks out the log-probability the model assigned to the token that actually occurred, from a distribution over the whole vocabulary.
  • tok_logp[n_ctx:] — only the reply is scored. Including the context would swamp the signal with how predictable other people's speeches are, which has nothing to do with the target.

NLL is measured in nats per token. The gaps this paper reports are around 0.02 nats, which sounds like nothing, and in absolute terms it is. §13 and §15 explain why the consistency of that gap across folds carries the result rather than its size.

11 · Bayesian optimization: searching when every guess is expensive

Now the search. We need the C that minimises held-out NLL, and we face a specific situation: the space is only ~12-dimensional and continuous, but each evaluation is expensive — a full re-merge plus dozens of forward passes through a 7B model. We can afford tens of evaluations, not millions. No gradients are available (the merge involves sign elections and hard masking).

That combination — expensive, low-dimensional, gradient-free — is the textbook case for Bayesian optimization.

How it works

BO keeps a probabilistic model of the objective, called a surrogate — here a Gaussian process. A GP predicts, for every unevaluated point, both a mean guess and an uncertainty, and its uncertainty is exactly zero where you have already measured. Then an acquisition function chooses where to look next by balancing:

  • Exploitation — go where the surrogate predicts a good value.
  • Exploration — go where the surrogate is unsure, since something better might hide there.

Coefficients are bounded to [0, 1.5]. The upper bound matters: §15 shows that an untuned all-ones merge is worse than not merging at all, so the search has to be able to pull weights down toward zero, and must not be allowed to run away upward.

12 · One trial, start to finish

Every piece is now on the table. This section follows a single BO trial through the actual call stack, so you can see how the sections connect. Step through it — each stage names the function that runs and what it hands to the next one.

Two things about this loop are worth naming explicitly, because they are what make the method affordable at all.

Nothing is trained, and nothing is reloaded. The model is loaded once. Every trial rewrites weights in place and rewrites them again next trial. Compare that to the obvious alternative — fine-tune the target 40 times with different settings — and the cost difference is the entire reason POLIS can run on 30 utterances.

The expensive part moved. At 0.5B the forward passes dominated. At 7B the re-merge does: writing ΔW for 4 anchors × 822M adapted parameters, every trial. That inversion is why the project's own runbook insists on a timed 5-trial calibration before committing to a full run — the old timings predict nothing.

13 · Nested cross-validation: not fooling yourself

Here is a subtle trap. If you tune 12 coefficients to minimise NLL on 30 sentences and then report that NLL, the number is meaningless — you optimised it directly. A held-out split fixes that, but with only 30 sentences the split is small and the estimate is noisy and luck-dependent.

Nested cross-validation is the clean answer. Split the budget into k folds. Hold one fold out, run the entire optimisation on the rest, then score on the untouched fold. Rotate through all folds and aggregate. Every fold's score comes from coefficients that never saw it. 

for f in range(folds):
    te  = [allinst[i] for i in fold_idx[f]]                              # untouched

    bs_name, bs_te = best_single(merger, scorer, names, dev, te, n_g)

The design detail that makes it fair Every baseline is rotated through identical folds — including best_single, which re-picks its winning anchor using only that fold's dev data. A baseline allowed to choose using the test fold would be getting information POLIS never had.

Paired vs unpaired — the statistic that decides the paper

Because every method is scored on the same five folds, the results are paired, and that changes which number you should look at. Here is the distinction, because it is the difference between "this works" and "this is noise" in §15.

Suppose method A scores [2.09, 2.24, 2.29] and method B scores [2.11, 2.25, 2.31]. Each list has a spread of about ±0.09 — far bigger than the ~0.02 gap between the two methods. Reported as "2.21 ± 0.09 vs 2.22 ± 0.09" the difference looks hopeless, buried in noise.

But look down the columns instead of across them. Fold 1: A wins by 0.02. Fold 2: A wins by 0.01. Fold 3: A wins by 0.02. The ±0.09 spread is not measurement error at all — it is some folds simply containing harder sentences than others, and both methods face exactly the same hard sentences. Subtract per fold and that shared difficulty cancels out, leaving [0.02, 0.01, 0.02]: a spread of ±0.005 around a mean of 0.017.

Rule of thumb When two methods are evaluated on the same data, always subtract first and average second. Averaging first throws away the pairing and inflates your apparent noise by whatever the variation between test items happens to be — which here is roughly tenfold. This mistake was actually made and caught mid-session on the real results; the unpaired reading said "inside noise", the paired reading said "5 of 5 folds".

14 · Scoring a survey — and a trap worth internalising

NLL is an internal proxy: it only says the model predicts the target's words well. The paper's external metric is different and deliberately untouchable — real politicians answered the UTAS academic survey, a set of policy statements each rated on a 5-point Likert scale from "agree" to "disagree", and those answers are never used in training or in the optimiser. Nothing in the pipeline can game them.

This section is how the survey is administered and scored. What it measured, and why that turned out to be the hardest question in the project, is §16.

To administer a survey to a model, you don't generate text and parse it — free generation fails in a hundred ways. Instead, score each candidate answer as a forced continuation and compare log-probabilities. No parsing, and you get a full distribution rather than one sample.

The length bug

The natural implementation is to sum log P over the answer's tokens and softmax the sums. This is broken, and understanding why is worth the detour.

Every token's log-probability is negative. So a longer answer accumulates a more negative total simply for being longer. In this survey the options are 「そう思う」 (short) through 「どちらかといえばそう思う」 (much longer) — so a softmax over summed log-probs collapses onto the shortest option almost regardless of content. You measure string length, not belief.

The fix is to divide by token count, giving average per-token log-probability:

logprobs.append(lp)
norm_logprobs.append(lp / max(n, 1))
# Softmax over LENGTH-NORMALISED log-probs. Option strings differ in token
# count, so a softmax over raw summed log-probs is biased toward shorter
# options and collapses degenerately.
probs = torch.softmax(norm_t, dim=0).tolist()
expectation = sum((i + 1) * p for i, p in enumerate(probs))

The expectation — Σ i·pi, each possible answer multiplied by its probability — is the payoff of scoring this way. It places the persona continuously on the 1–5 scale rather than forcing a discrete pick, so "leans agree" and "strongly agrees" are distinguishable. You then compare that number to the politician's real coded answer and average the gap across all 33 items, which gives mean absolute error (MAE) — literally "on average, how many scale points off were we". MAE of 0 is perfect; MAE of 1 means you are typically one Likert step out.

The second scorer, and why there had to be one

Length-normalising fixes the bias toward short options but introduces a subtler problem. After dividing by token count, all five options end up with per-token log-probabilities sitting in a narrow band — they are all fluent Japanese phrases, so none of them is very surprising to the model. Softmax over five near-identical numbers gives five near-equal probabilities, and the expectation Σ i·pi of a near-uniform distribution over 1–5 is… almost exactly 3. The metric collapses toward the midpoint whatever the model actually believes.

The alternative is numeric-label scoring: don't score the phrase 「そう思う」, score the single token "1", and let the system prompt carry the mapping. One token per option, so length normalisation is moot, and the five candidates are genuinely distinguishable to the model.

That is the headline. §16 is what happened to the other metric.

16 · When the metric broke: an anatomy of a null result

NLL is an internal measure — it says the merged model predicts the target's words well. The claim readers actually care about is bigger: does the simulated politician hold the right opinions? That is what the UTAS survey (§14) was for, and it is where the project spent most of its second day. The answer turned out to be no, and the way that was established is more instructive than the result.

Step 1 · Compare against something stupid

So a dummy was added: a "model" that ignores the question, ignores the politician, and answers 3 — the exact scale midpoint — to all 33 items. Note that the numeric scorer did fix the thing it was designed to fix — that is the frustrating part. Compare two ways of reading the same output: MAE computed from the expectation Σ i·pi, and MAE computed from the single most likely answer. If the probability mass is smeared across all five options, those two disagree, because the expectation drifts to the middle while the argmax does not. On target 高市 under verbose scoring they were 1.330 and 1.545 — smeared. Under numeric scoring, 1.406 and 1.515 — nearly the same number. The model now commits to an answer. It is simply confidently wrong more often than the midpoint guess is accidentally right.

Why a stupid baseline is not optional The midpoint dummy wins for a boring structural reason: real survey answers cluster near the middle, so "always say 3" is never far off. Any metric where a constant is competitive cannot distinguish a good model from a bad one, and you will not discover that by staring at your model's scores. Add the dumbest possible baseline before you interpret anything.

There is exactly one place where the models come out ahead, and it is the exception that proves the rule. 赤嶺's answers are bimodal — 14 of his 33 sit at one end of the scale and 8 at the other, with only 7 in the middle — so "always say 3" is an unusually bad guess for him. On the within-1 column (fraction of items landing within one scale point of the truth), every configuration beats the dummy, 0.455–0.667 against 0.333. The dummy is not strong; the models are just weaker than a lucky structural artefact almost everywhere else.

Step 2 · Separate "wrong persona" from "no persona"

Losing to a constant tells you the numbers are bad, but not why. There are two very different diagnoses, and they call for opposite responses:

Diagnosis A — wrong persona

The persona changes when you change who it is, but lands in the wrong place. The merge is doing something; it is doing it badly. Response: improve the merge.

Diagnosis B — no persona

The persona doesn't change at all when you change who it is. The survey answers are a property of the instrument, not of the politician. Response: stop reporting the metric — it cannot support a claim in either direction.

A one-target MAE cannot tell these apart. Distinguishing them needed a different experiment, and two facts made it cheap. First, the persona is conditioned on the politician's name alone — the system prompt says "you are Diet member X" and nothing else — so administering the survey needs no speech data, no adapter and no merge. Second, the survey wave contains 594 politicians with complete 33-item answer vectors, and only 3 had ever been used.

So: run the base 7B model as all 594 personas and administer the survey to each. Total cost, 10.7 minutes.

Step 3 · The population sweep

With 594 simulated answer vectors and 594 real ones, two questions become answerable.

Name-swap divergence. How much do the simulated answers move when the only thing you change is the name in the prompt — compared to how much the real politicians differ from each other? If simulations vary as much as real people do, the persona is carrying identity. If they barely vary, it isn't.

Take two politicians at random and average the gap between their answers, item by item, on the 1–5 scale. Real politicians differ by 1.13 scale points per item. Their simulations differ by 0.24 — a ratio of 0.209.

Not literally inert: of the 19,900 persona pairs, none produce identical answers. But changing the name moves the answers about a fifth as much as real politicians differ. Item by item it is starker — the median item has a standard deviation of 0.186 across all 594 personas against 1.013 for the real answers, and 9 of the 33 items have a standard deviation below 0.05, meaning the model returns effectively the same answer no matter whose name you put in the prompt.

Rank of the true politician. A cleverer test, designed to survive exactly the failure mode that sank MAE. Take the vector simulated for politician X. Measure its distance to all 594 real vectors and sort them. Where does the real X come in that ranking? Only the ordering matters, so it does not care that everything clusters near the midpoint — it only asks whether X's simulation leans toward the real X relative to everyone else.

Chance is the 50th percentile. Measured: median 52.1%, against a floor of 50.1% established by constant vectors. Top-1 accuracy is 0.002, which is exactly 1/594 — one lucky hit. The model cannot identify which politician it is simulating.

A design error caught by a known-answer fixture, then corrected again The first version of this metric was checked on a small fixture, which reported that a constant vector scores the 68.4th percentile — well above chance — and a plausible-sounding explanation was written down: a constant vector sits near the population centroid, so it is closer than average to everyone. That explanation was wrong. On the full sweep the constants land at exactly 50.1%, as they must: a name-independent vector induces one fixed ordering of the 594 candidates, so as the persona ranges over the whole set its rank takes every value exactly once. The 68.4% was sampling noise from a 60-persona fixture. A number that confirms a story you find satisfying still needs the sanity check that would refute it.

The skew that explains both. Simulated answers have a mean of 2.271; real answers average 2.763. Every persona leans "agree", regardless of who it is. The three targets make this concrete:

What this rules out The failure is not in the merge, and not in the low-resource approximation. It is in the step before both: a model fine-tuned on twenty thousand of a politician's own speeches still cannot answer that politician's survey. Speech style and stated policy positions are not connected by this pipeline at any resource level. Improving the merge could not have fixed it, and neither can more target data.
Decision: do not train the anchors on survey answers This is the tempting fix and it has to be refused, for a reason worth understanding precisely. It would not be label leakage — the anchors' answers are not the targets' answers, and no target's ground truth would ever be seen. But the 33 items are identical across politicians, so training on them teaches the task format, the meaning of the 1–5 scale, and the population's overall answer distribution. The claim would quietly degrade from "personas fitted to speech encode ideology that an independent instrument can read" to "models trained on surveys can answer surveys", and the merge would then transport survey-answering competence — masking the very distinction this whole session was built to establish. It is legitimate only as a labelled diagnostic, an upper bound on whether the merge can transport ideology at all. Never as evidence for the method.
Decision: stop work on the UTAS metric Two scorers, a 594-person population control, a rank metric and a full-resource anchor self-test all agree that this protocol does not carry politician identity at this scale — with or without the merge, and with or without abundant data. The correct move is not a third scorer. The paper reports the survey result as a limitation with the diagnostic attached, and the headline rests on NLL. Diagnosis B, and the response that goes with it.
Strength caveat — stated because it cuts against the conclusion too Per-target rank is a single draw and these numbers are noisy. Three targets cannot carry a p-value: "all three below chance" is p ≈ 0.125 on sign alone. So the claim is no evidence that the merge injects identity — not proof that it doesn't. This is a real difference, and it is one more argument for the n≈30 expansion in §17.

The finding that survives all of this

There is one positive result hiding in the wreckage, and it is arguably the most interesting thing in the paper. NLL and the survey metric are anti-correlated. The uniform merge has by far the worst NLL (2.74–2.99 against a base of 2.25–2.53) yet posts the best or near-best survey score on two targets — and POLIS, which wins on NLL every time, is never the best configuration on the survey.

Optimising how well a model predicts someone's speech does not transfer to getting their stated policy positions right. Those are different targets, and improving one can cost you the other. That is a genuine limit of weight-space persona composition, and stating it plainly is worth more than burying an uninterpretable table.

17 · What stands, what doesn't, and what to do next

ClaimStatus
Tuned merging beats the alternatives on held-out NLL Stands. Confirmed at 7B, nested CV, paired, 14/15 folds against the hardest baseline. Small margins, consistent sign.
Tuning the coefficients is what matters Stands. The untuned uniform merge is worse than not merging.
Per-depth granularity helps Refuted. Null at 0.5B, tighter null at 7B. The "it will show up at scale" hedge has been tested and retired.
The merge recovers ideology, not just register Unsupported. The best single anchor is uncorrelated with the target's party, and the survey metric shows no identity signal at all.
Simulated personas answer surveys like the real politician No evidence. Loses to a constant in 12/12 cells; rank at chance. §16.
Speech data can be turned into survey positions at all Not with this pipeline. The anchor self-test breaks upstream of everything else: 1 of 4 fully-trained anchors, on 12k–20k of their own speeches, beats a constant on their own survey.

That last row is the one to sit with, because it reorders the others. The survey failure is not a limitation of low-resource simulation, of the merge, or of n=3 — it is present at full resource with no merge at all. Whatever fixes it is upstream of everything this paper builds.

The limitation that bounds all of the above: n = 3

Every result in §15 and §16 rests on three politicians. Per-fold consistency is not a substitute for more people — folds are slices of one person's speech, so five folds give you five looks at the same individual, not five independent tests of the method. Three targets cannot carry a population claim in either direction.

Fixing this is cheap and is the top of the queue. It requires no training at all — just preference pairs per target and about ten minutes of GPU each. 633 politicians appear in both the 2024 survey wave and the organised speech corpus, 437 of them with at least a megabyte of text. Scaling from 3 to ~30 is a day of compute.

And a framing problem the expansion has to fix Ranked by speech volume among those 633, the three current targets are #9 (枝野), #17 (高市) and #20 (赤嶺) — the top 3% of the corpus. The paper is about low-resource simulation, and it is currently being tested on some of the most verbose politicians in the Diet, artificially restricted to 30 utterances. Sampling the next batch of targets from ranks 200–600 raises n and puts the method on its actual thesis. Expect the margin to shrink. A smaller margin on genuinely data-poor politicians is the stronger result.

Deferred: more anchors

The obvious other axis is anchor count — 4 today, and the original design assumed 8. There is one real argument for it: the granularity null might be an artefact of having only four sources to blend, since with so few directions there may be nothing for per-depth weighting to differentiate. But each anchor is a full training run and ~3.3 GB of resident VRAM, so eight anchors need 26.4 GB of deltas plus a 15.2 GB model — over the limit of a 48 GB card. Speculative and expensive, against targets which are cheap and bound every claim in the paper. Targets first.

The general shape of this The interesting work in this project was not the method — that was mostly assembling known pieces. It was the instrumentation: adding a dumb baseline, running a 594-person control, inventing a metric that survives the failure mode that killed the previous one, testing the chain at maximum resource before trying to improve it at minimum, and correcting two statistical mistakes that each pointed the conclusion the wrong way. Notice that four of those five are attempts to break the result rather than to strengthen it. A result you cannot get a null out of is a result you cannot trust.

18 · Engineering traps worth more than they look

The silent no-op

A 7B model in bf16 needs ~15 GB. On an 8 GB GPU, device_map="auto" helpfully splits it, leaving overflow layers as meta tensors — placeholders that carry shape but no storage, materialised on demand during a forward pass.

Forward passes work fine. But the merge writes weights in place, and an in-place write to a meta tensor is silently discarded. The result: merges that appear to succeed, coefficients for the upper layers that do nothing, and an optimiser cheerfully searching a partly-flat objective for hours. Even the reversibility check passed — a no-op write reverses to a no-op.

The fix is to refuse to run rather than to run wrongly:

meta = [p for p in self.modules
        if self._params[p + ".weight"].device.type == "meta"]
if meta:
    raise RuntimeError(
        f"{len(meta)} of {len(self.modules)} target modules are meta tensors "
        f"(offloaded) ... In-place merge writes would silently no-op.")

research/polis/merge_layer_group.py:98–106

Generalisable lesson The dangerous bugs in ML systems are rarely crashes — they are the ones that return plausible numbers. When an operation can be silently skipped, assert that it happened.

Memory that scales the wrong way

The merger keeps every anchor's reconstructed ΔW resident as a dense fp32 tensor. That is cheap at 0.5B and becomes the dominant cost quickly, because ΔW has the shape of the full weight matrix even though it came from a rank-16 factorisation:

Model weights (bf16) Dense fp32 anchor deltas — the part that scales badly

Dense fp32 deltas for 4 anchors, plus the bf16 model and its snapshots. At 32B the deltas alone exceed the model itself.

The fix follows from a detail of apply(): it already iterates module by module, so all the deltas never need to exist at once. Keep only the small LoRA factors and rebuild each module's ΔW inside the loop — recomputing B·A costs milliseconds on a GPU and drops 32B from ~129 GB to ~69 GB, which is the difference between four cards and one.

What actually went wrong on the day

The 7B run in §15 was a rented GPU on a two-hour clock, which is a good stress test of how much of a research pipeline is real. Roughly the first hour went to these four:

SymptomCause, and what it teaches
FileNotFoundError on a data file, in one script out of three That script's --dpo-dir defaulted to the full corpus, which lives on a laptop SSD and was never copied to the rented box. The other two scripts were fine because the shell runner set the path explicitly. A default that works on your machine is a landmine on someone else's.
Out of memory on a card that had just run the same model fine The script built a throwaway merger object purely to read n_layers off it, never freed it, then built another. Each one pins ~14.8 GB. Two of those plus a 15.2 GB model overruns 48 GB. Reading the layer count off the adapter file instead dropped peak usage to ~30 GB. In GPU code, "I just need one field from it" still allocates the whole object.
The runbook and the pasted commands disagreed about --device An uncommitted edit removing --device cuda had been sitting in the working tree and got committed without anyone reading the diff. Review your own diff before committing, especially the parts you did not write today.
Ran the wrong 52-minute job instead of the right 12-minute one The instruction was "the snippet below section 6". There were fifteen runnable blocks and no way to name one unambiguously. They all have IDs now. If a runbook has more than a handful of commands, they need names.
Output written to the filesystem root, three separate times Several runbook blocks set a shell variable on their first line and use it below. Paste a block starting from line two and the variable is unset — and an unset variable in bash expands to nothing, so "$ART/run.log" silently becomes /run.log. The error you eventually see is "no such file", which sends you hunting for a missing file rather than a missing variable. Prefer literal paths in a runbook, or set -u so an unset variable is an error rather than an empty string.

19 · Run it yourself

Everything above is runnable. All the code lives in one directory, research/polis/, and the whole pipeline works at 0.5B scale on a laptop — which is exactly why the development study is at 0.5B.

The one-minute version

Each module has a self-test that needs no data beyond the adapters. Start here, because it proves the merge is real: 

# does the merge apply, reverse exactly, and respond to per-group coefficients?
python merge_layer_group.py --device cuda

# are the anchors actually pointing in different directions?
python anchor_delta_cosine.py

# can the model answer a Likert item at all?
python polis_option_logprob.py --demo
Read the self-test output, don't just check it exits 0 merge_layer_group.py prints four NLLs — base, uniform, upper-group-only, lower-group-only — plus a restored base. They must be four distinct numbers and the restore must match base exactly. If they collapse, the merge is silently no-oping (§18) and every hour after that is wasted. Printing [OK] is not sufficient; that line is reached either way.

The full pipeline, in order

# 1. pick anchors (writes data/data/polis/anchors.json)
python select_polis_anchors.py --max-anchors 4

# 2. extract prompt/chosen pairs for one anchor
python export_polis_dpo_pairs.py --person-id 152 --k 4 --max-pairs 1000

# 3. fill in the `rejected` caricature (needs a Gemini API key)
python generate_polis_rejected.py --person-id 152

# 4. train that anchor's adapter
python train_one_politician_persona.py \
    --data ../../data/data/polis/dpo_pairs_full/152.jsonl \
    --output output/polis_152_kishida --epochs 1

# 5. ... repeat 2-4 for 2377, 3631, 5520, then optimise the merge
python bo_merge_coeffs.py --target 1279 --budget 30 --trials 40 \
    --dpo-dir ../../data/data/polis/dpo_pairs_targets

Step 5 is the experiment. Add --nested for the unbiased cross-validated version (§13), or --utas-eval with a wave file to get the external survey metric (§14) alongside the NLL — and if you do, add --utas-numeric, because the default scorer is the one §14 explains is broken.

The diagnostics from §16

These are the two scripts that turned an uninterpretable survey table into a conclusion, and they are the cheapest things in the repository. Neither trains anything; the first needs a GPU for ten minutes, the second runs on a laptop in seconds. 

# administer the survey to all 594 politicians as name-only personas
python utas_population_eval.py \
    --base Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct --scoring numeric \
    --gt ../../data/data/polis/utas_ground_truth/2024HoR.json \

If you take one habit from this walkthrough, take this one: before believing any evaluation number, run the version of it that has no model in it at all.
