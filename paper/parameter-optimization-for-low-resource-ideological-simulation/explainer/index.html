POLIS: composing personas in weight space

How do you simulate a politician who has barely said anything on the record? This is a guided tour of the actual code behind the POLIS paper — every concept paired with the lines that implement it. Assumes you know attention, MLPs, and how a language model is trained; everything past that is explained from scratch.

1 · The problem: you cannot fine-tune on data you don't have

"Silicon sampling" is the idea of using an LLM to stand in for a human respondent — ask the model to role-play a person and record its answers as if they were survey data. The failure mode is immediate and well-documented: ask a model to be a politician and it produces a caricature assembled from media coverage, not that person's actual positions.

Fine-tuning on the person's own words fixes this — if the words exist. In the Japanese Diet, a handful of party leaders have tens of thousands of transcribed utterances, while most backbenchers have almost none. That long tail is exactly who a representative simulation needs, and exactly where per-person fine-tuning is impossible.

The reframe: locate, don't train

POLIS treats ideology as a continuous space and the weight-changes from fine-tuning as composable vectors in that space. Train adapters only on the few data-rich politicians ("anchors"). For a sparse target, don't train anything — find the blend of anchors that best explains the handful of sentences the target did say. The target's data is used to locate a point, not to fit a model. With 4 anchors and 3 depth groups that's 12 numbers to determine, which is a very different statistical problem from fine-tuning billions of parameters on 30 sentences.

2 · Building the data: session-grounded preference pairs

Before any modelling, we need training examples that capture how a specific politician actually talks. The trick here is session grounding: rather than isolated quotes, each example reconstructs the real conversational context from the Diet transcript.

For every real utterance by an anchor, one training instance is emitted:

FieldContent
promptThe last K speeches before this utterance in the same sitting, plus a cue for the anchor to speak
chosenWhat the politician actually said next
rejectedA strong LLM (Gemini 2.5-flash-lite) role-playing that same politician on the same context
Why this matters The rejected side is the paper's argument made literal. The model isn't just learning "sound like a politician" — it's learning "the real person, not the LLM's stereotype of them." The thing the paper criticises is the exact negative the training signal pushes away from.

The immediately preceding speech is always kept intact even when truncating, because it is usually the question being answered — drop it and the reply becomes incoherent. 

# prompt = (optional persona instruction) + last K speeches preceding the
#          utterance in the same sitting + a cue for the anchor to speak,
#          truncated to a char budget but always keeping the immediately
#          preceding speech intact.

Context is capped at ~1200 characters as a proxy for the trainer's 1024-token limit (Japanese runs roughly one token per character). In practice the resulting prompts are ~1,336 tokens at the median — a number that comes back to haunt the compute budget later.

3 · DPO: learning from pairs without a reward model

Direct Preference Optimization is how each anchor adapter is trained. If you know RLHF, DPO is the shortcut that skips it: classic RLHF trains a separate reward model on human preferences, then optimises the policy against it with RL. DPO proves you can collapse those two steps into a single supervised-looking loss.

L = − log σ( β · [ log πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] )

Reading it piece by piece:

  • πθ is the model being trained; πref is a frozen copy of where it started.
  • yw ("win") is the real utterance; yl ("lose") is the caricature.
  • Each ratio asks: has this response become more likely than it was before training? Dividing by the reference is what keeps the model from wandering off — it can only be rewarded for moving relatively.
  • The difference of the two ratios is a score: high when the model has raised the real utterance and lowered the caricature. σ turns it into a probability and the log makes it a loss.
  • β controls how far the model may drift from the reference.

Two diagnostics get reported during training, and both appear in the project logs: rewards/accuracies (how often the real utterance scores above the caricature — reached 1.0) and rewards/margins (by how much — reached 7–9). The four 7B anchors all trained cleanly to these values.

Careful High margins mean the adapter reliably separates real from fake. That is not the same as having learned ideology. At 0.5B the adapters largely captured register — the cadence of Diet speech — which is why the strongest-trained anchor kept winning as a generic "fluent-parliamentarian" backbone regardless of the target's party. See §12.

4 · LoRA and the idea of a "task vector"

Fine-tuning every weight of a 7B model per politician is unaffordable. LoRA freezes the base model and learns a low-rank correction instead. For a weight matrix W ∈ ℝout×in, it learns two thin matrices A ∈ ℝr×in and B ∈ ℝout×r with r = 16, and the effective weight becomes W + ΔW where:

ΔW = (α / r) · B · A

B·A is an out×in matrix — the same shape as W — but it is built from only r(out+in) parameters instead of out·in. At 7B this makes each anchor adapter a 20 MB file standing in for a 15 GB model. α/r is a fixed scaling constant from the config (α=32, r=16, so the scale is 2).

Here is the conceptual leap the whole paper rests on. Once training is done, ΔW is just a matrix — a direction in weight space that means "be more like Kishida." It can be added, scaled, and combined like any vector. This is the task vector view, and it is what makes "average two personas" a meaningful operation rather than a category error. 

for pfx in prefixes:
    a = next(v for k, v in weights.items() if k.startswith(pfx) and ".lora_A" in k)
    b = next(v for k, v in weights.items() if k.startswith(pfx) and ".lora_B" in k)
    delta = (b.float() @ a.float()) * scaling            # [out, in]

Does the "space" actually have room?

A blend of anchors can only express positions the anchors span. If all four ΔW pointed the same way, there would be nothing to interpolate — one shared direction with slightly different magnitudes. So the first thing the project checked was the pairwise cosine similarity between anchor task vectors (1.0 = identical direction, 0 = orthogonal).

Measured at 7B across 822 million adapted parameters per anchor (anchor_delta_cosine.py). Everything is far below the 0.9 danger line, so the anchors genuinely span a space.

The interesting part Look at the structure, not just the magnitudes. Every left-party pair (≈0.088) is more similar to each other than any of them is to the LDP anchor (≈0.064). At 0.5B these numbers were uniform regardless of party. Ideology is showing up as geometry — but note this is 3 pairs against 3 pairs, so it is suggestive, not a significance test.

5 · DARE: drop most of the vector, keep the meaning

When you add several task vectors together they interfere — unrelated coordinates pile up into noise that degrades the model. DARE (Drop And REscale) is a surprisingly blunt fix: randomly delete ~90% of each task vector's entries, then multiply the survivors by 1/density to compensate.

keep with probability d · then multiply by 1/d

Why this doesn't destroy the adapter: the rescaling preserves the expected value of every entry (drop 90%, multiply the rest by 10, and the average is unchanged). Fine-tuning deltas turn out to be highly redundant, so a random 10% carries nearly the same information — while the collisions between different anchors become far rarer, since two sparse vectors rarely have non-zeros in the same place. 

mask = (torch.rand(delta.shape, generator=gen) < self.density)
delta = torch.where(mask, delta / self.density, torch.zeros_like(delta))

merge_layer_group.py:84–85

Why generator=gen is load-bearing The mask is drawn from a seeded generator fixed once at construction. If the mask were redrawn on each merge, the same coefficients would give a different result every time — the optimiser in §9 would be chasing a moving target it cannot model. Freezing the randomness makes the objective a deterministic function of the coefficients. This is a general principle worth absorbing: stochastic components must be frozen before you optimise through them.

6 · TIES: settle the disagreements by vote

DARE reduces collisions but doesn't resolve them. When two anchors do both want to change the same weight in opposite directions, naive averaging cancels them into mush — a weight that means something to both anchors ends up meaning nothing.

TIES resolves this per-coordinate with an election. For each individual weight: sum the (already coefficient-weighted) contributions to decide a winning sign, discard every anchor that disagrees with that sign, and average only the survivors. A coordinate ends up decisively positive or decisively negative — never averaged into zero. 

def _ties(deltas):
    """TIES disjoint merge over already-weighted deltas [n_anchors, out, in]."""
    elected = torch.sign(deltas.sum(dim=0))                  # winning sign per weight
    agree   = (torch.sign(deltas) == elected) & (deltas != 0)  # who voted with it
    kept    = torch.where(agree, deltas, torch.zeros_like(deltas))
    count   = agree.sum(dim=0).clamp(min=1)
    return kept.sum(dim=0) / count                        # mean of the winners only

merge_layer_group.py:132–139

Note clamp(min=1) — it guards the divide-by-zero when a coordinate is zero in every anchor (very common after a 90% drop). And the average is over agreeing anchors only, so a coordinate touched by one anchor keeps that anchor's full magnitude rather than being diluted by three zeros.

7 · Layer groups: the knob that makes this a search problem

So far a merge needs one weight per anchor. POLIS's contribution is that one number per anchor is not enough. Transformer layers specialise: earlier layers handle more lexical and syntactic structure, later layers more abstract and task-specific content. A persona is not uniformly distributed across depth — so the amount of each anchor you want to inject should vary by depth.

Layers are partitioned into G contiguous depth groups, and the coefficient becomes a matrix C ∈ ℝK×G — one value per (anchor, group) pair. With 4 anchors and 3 groups that is 12 free parameters, the entire "model" being fit to the sparse target. 

def _group_of(self, mod_path):
    layer = int(m.group(1)) if m else 0
    # contiguous depth partition: floor(layer / ceil(n_layers / n_groups))
    per = -(-self._n_layers // self.n_groups)  # ceil div
    return min(layer // per, self.n_groups - 1)

merge_layer_group.py:103–108

Why custom code was needed The standard library (PEFT) offers add_weighted_adapter(combination_type="dare_ties"), but it only supports one global weight per adapter. The per-depth coefficient matrix is precisely what it cannot express, so the merge is reimplemented as direct tensor arithmetic. That is the reason merge_layer_group.py exists at all.

The merge, end to end

apply(C) is called once per optimiser trial. It restores the base weights, rebuilds the merge under the new coefficients, and writes the result in place — so a single loaded model is reused for hundreds of evaluations with no retraining and no reloading. 

for mod_path, slot in self.modules.items():
    base = self.base_weights[mod_path]
    stacked = []
    for ai, am in enumerate(slot):
        w = float(C[ai, am.group])          # ← coefficient for THIS anchor at THIS depth
        if w != 0.0:
            stacked.append(am.delta * w)
    merged = self._ties(torch.stack(stacked, dim=0))
    self._params[mod_path + ".weight"].data.copy_(
        (base.float() + merged.to(base.device)).to(base.dtype))

merge_layer_group.py:111–130

The preset is the structure the optimiser actually discovered for the LDP target: keep the ideologically-matched anchor, zero the distant ones, and vary the amount by depth — a pattern a single global weight per anchor literally cannot represent.

8 · The objective: how well does this persona predict real sentences?

To search over C we need a number that says how good a merge is. POLIS uses negative log-likelihood of the target's real utterances, given their real session context. In plain terms: show the merged model the room, and ask how surprised it is by what this person actually said. Lower is better. 

def mean_nll(self, instances):
    for prompt, cont in instances:
        ids_p = self.tok(prompt, ...).input_ids       # session context
        ids_c = self.tok(cont, ...).input_ids         # what they really said
        ids = torch.cat([ids_p, ids_c], dim=1)
        logits = self.model(ids).logits[:, :-1]
        logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)
        tok_logp = logp.gather(-1, ids[:, 1:].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)[0]
        n_ctx = ids_p.shape[1] - 1
        tot += -tok_logp[n_ctx:].mean().item()        # ← score ONLY the reply

bo_merge_coeffs.py:76–89

Three details that are easy to skim past but matter:

  • logits[:, :-1] and ids[:, 1:] — the standard off-by-one of causal LMs. Position t's logits predict token t+1, so predictions and targets must be shifted against each other.
  • gather picks out the log-probability the model assigned to the token that actually occurred, from a distribution over the whole vocabulary.
  • tok_logp[n_ctx:] — only the reply is scored. Including the context would swamp the signal with how predictable other people's speeches are, which has nothing to do with the target.

NLL is measured in nats per token. A drop of 0.07 nats sounds tiny, and in absolute terms it is; §12 explains why the consistency of that gap matters more than its size.

9 · Bayesian optimization: searching when every guess is expensive

Now the search. We need the C that minimises held-out NLL, and we face a specific situation: the space is only ~12-dimensional and continuous, but each evaluation is expensive — a full re-merge plus dozens of forward passes through a 7B model. We can afford tens of evaluations, not millions. No gradients are available (the merge involves sign elections and hard masking).

That combination — expensive, low-dimensional, gradient-free — is the textbook case for Bayesian optimization.

How it works

BO keeps a probabilistic model of the objective, called a surrogate — here a Gaussian process. A GP predicts, for every unevaluated point, both a mean guess and an uncertainty, and its uncertainty is exactly zero where you have already measured. Then an acquisition function chooses where to look next by balancing:

  • Exploitation — go where the surrogate predicts a good value.
  • Exploration — go where the surrogate is unsure, since something better might hide there.

def objective(trial):

Coefficients are bounded to [0, 1.5]. The upper bound matters: §12 shows that an untuned all-ones merge is worse than not merging at all, so the search has to be able to pull weights down toward zero, and must not be allowed to run away upward.

10 · Nested cross-validation: not fooling yourself

Here is a subtle trap. If you tune 12 coefficients to minimise NLL on 30 sentences and then report that NLL, the number is meaningless — you optimised it directly. A held-out split fixes that, but with only 30 sentences the split is small and the estimate is noisy and luck-dependent.

Nested cross-validation is the clean answer. Split the budget into k folds. Hold one fold out, run the entire optimisation on the rest, then score on the untouched fold. Rotate through all folds and aggregate. Every fold's score comes from coefficients that never saw it. 

for f in range(folds):
    te  = [allinst[i] for i in fold_idx[f]]                              # untouched
    dev = [allinst[i] for j in range(folds) if j != f for i in fold_idx[j]]

    merger.restore();            rows["base"].append(scorer.mean_nll(te))
    merger.apply(np.ones(...));  rows["uniform"].append(scorer.mean_nll(te))
    bs_name, bs_te = best_single(merger, scorer, names, dev, te, n_g)

    bestC, _, _ = bo_bestC(merger, scorer, dev, ...)   # full BO, inside the fold
    merger.apply(bestC)
    rows["BO"].append(scorer.mean_nll(te))

bo_merge_coeffs.py:147–176

The design detail that makes it fair Every baseline is rotated through identical folds — including best_single, which re-picks its winning anchor using only that fold's dev data. A baseline allowed to choose using the test fold would be getting information POLIS never had. Because all methods see the same folds, the results are paired, and per-fold differences can be compared directly, which is far more sensitive than comparing two averages.

11 · Scoring a survey — and a trap worth internalising

NLL is an internal proxy. The paper's external metric is different and deliberately untouchable: real politicians answered the UTAS academic survey (5-point Likert policy items), and those answers are never used in training or in the optimiser. Nothing in the pipeline can game them.

To administer a survey to a model, you don't generate text and parse it — free generation fails in a hundred ways. Instead, score each candidate answer as a forced continuation and compare log-probabilities. No parsing, and you get a full distribution rather than one sample.

The length bug

The natural implementation is to sum log P over the answer's tokens and softmax the sums. This is broken, and understanding why is worth the detour.

Every token's log-probability is negative. So a longer answer accumulates a more negative total simply for being longer. In this survey the options are 「そう思う」 (short) through 「どちらかといえばそう思う」 (much longer) — so a softmax over summed log-probs collapses onto the shortest option almost regardless of content. You measure string length, not belief.

The fix is to divide by token count, giving average per-token log-probability:

logprobs.append(lp)
norm_logprobs.append(lp / max(n, 1))
# Softmax over LENGTH-NORMALISED log-probs. Option strings differ in token
# count, so a softmax over raw summed log-probs is biased toward shorter
# options and collapses degenerately.
probs = torch.softmax(norm_t, dim=0).tolist()
expectation = sum((i + 1) * p for i, p in enumerate(probs))

The expectation — Σ i·pi — is the payoff of scoring this way. It places the persona continuously on the 1–5 scale rather than forcing a discrete pick, so "leans agree" and "strongly agrees" are distinguishable and you can take a mean absolute error against the politician's real coded answer.

Scale-gating: an honest negative result At 0.5B this metric is a degenerate floor. The expectation sits pinned near 3.0 no matter what you merge, so every method scores the same. A numeric-label variant (score the token "1".."5") failed differently — a first-option bias so severe the model agreed at p≈0.98 that consumption tax should be raised to 100%. The capability simply isn't there at 0.5B. At 7B it appears: the argmax spans the full scale and responds to content. The lesson is that an evaluation metric can require more model capability than the thing being evaluated.

Three things this shows

1 · Tuning does the work. The uniform all-ones merge is dramatically worse than not merging at all — more than a full nat. Untuned task vectors overshoot and corrupt the model. Every bit of POLIS's advantage comes from choosing coefficients, which is the cleanest possible evidence that the optimisation stage is load-bearing rather than decorative.

2 · The gains are small but relentless. POLIS beats the best single anchor by only 0.07–0.09 nats — but it does so in 15 out of 15 cross-validation folds, and beats the base model in 14 of 15. With paired folds, that consistency is the real result; the absolute magnitude is limited by what 0.5B adapters can encode.

3 · And the honest caveat. At 0.5B the adapters encode register more than ideology. The tell: the Kishida anchor was selected as best-single in all 15 folds — including for the Communist and Constitutional-Democratic targets. It was not acting as an ideology, it was acting as a generic "fluent Diet speech" backbone, because it happened to be the most cleanly trained. The optimiser then added target-specific residual in the other depth groups. Clean ideological recovery is a claim about larger models, and the paper says so rather than overselling the small one.

13 · Two engineering traps worth more than they look

The silent no-op

A 7B model in bf16 needs ~15 GB. On an 8 GB GPU, device_map="auto" helpfully splits it, leaving overflow layers as meta tensors — placeholders that carry shape but no storage, materialised on demand during a forward pass.

Forward passes work fine. But the merge writes weights in place, and an in-place write to a meta tensor is silently discarded. The result: merges that appear to succeed, coefficients for the upper layers that do nothing, and an optimiser cheerfully searching a partly-flat objective for hours. Even the reversibility check passed — a no-op write reverses to a no-op.

The fix is to refuse to run rather than to run wrongly:

meta = [p for p in self.modules
        if self._params[p + ".weight"].device.type == "meta"]
if meta:
    raise RuntimeError(
        f"{len(meta)} of {len(self.modules)} target modules are meta tensors "
        f"(offloaded) ... In-place merge writes would silently no-op.")

merge_layer_group.py:92–104

Generalisable lesson The dangerous bugs in ML systems are rarely crashes — they are the ones that return plausible numbers. When an operation can be silently skipped, assert that it happened.

Memory that scales the wrong way

The merger keeps every anchor's reconstructed ΔW resident as a dense fp32 tensor. That is cheap at 0.5B and becomes the dominant cost quickly, because ΔW has the shape of the full weight matrix even though it came from a rank-16 factorisation:

Model weights (bf16) Dense fp32 anchor deltas — the part that scales badly

Dense fp32 deltas for 4 anchors, plus the bf16 model and its snapshots. At 32B the deltas alone exceed the model itself.

The fix follows from a detail of apply(): it already iterates module by module, so all the deltas never need to exist at once. Keep only the small LoRA factors and rebuild each module's ΔW inside the loop — recomputing B·A costs milliseconds on a GPU and drops 32B from ~129 GB to ~69 GB, which is the difference between four cards and one.
